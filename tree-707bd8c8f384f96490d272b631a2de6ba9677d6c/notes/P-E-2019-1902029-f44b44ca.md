## Templates this paper hands you

**Which section takes which exhibit.** Allocation is strictly by stage of argument and it
is the cleanest schedule in the lane so far: the notation section takes the symbol table
(1.3), the model takes the flowchart and the equivalents matrix (3.3.2 Figure 1, 3.3.3
Figure 2), the calculation takes the province grid and the consistency scatter (3.5.1
Figure 3, 3.5.2 Figure 4), the evaluation section takes a THREE-EXHIBIT PAGE (3.5.3
Figures 5, 6 and 7 - the capacity chart, its classification, and the same capacity
renormalised by GDP), each project takes its value grid plus its advice charts (4.1.2
Table 2, 4.1.4 Figures 8 and 9; 4.2.2 Figure 10; 5.2.1 Figures 13 and 14), the seasonal and
annual sections take one chart each, and the appendix absorbs seven exhibits in three
pages. What to copy: one page can carry three exhibits if they are the same idea at three
normalisations, because the reader compares them in one glance - Figures 5, 6 and 7 go
absolute capacity, classified capacity, capacity per unit GDP, and that triplet is the
paper's best structural move. What not to copy: the appendix as a dumping ground with no
pointer. Seven exhibits live there and the body never names one of them; the three
"see the appendix" sentences name no figure, so the seven are effectively unindexed.

**When three dimensions appear.** Never, and the paper substitutes two cheaper tricks.
First, COLOUR AS A LEVEL: Figure 1's nine boxes encode hierarchy tier by fill colour with
no key at all, so the drawing is unreadable in greyscale; Figure 6 encodes its five ESC
bands as row fills, which is the one place the colour does decodable work (and is the
paper's only zebra shading). Second, NORMALISATION AS A THIRD AXIS: instead of adding a z
axis for a third quantity, the paper reissues the same chart divided by area (Figure 5)
and by GDP (Figure 7) - two exhibits instead of one 3-D box, and the reader gets an
ordinal comparison in each. Copy the reissue; do not copy the unkeyed colour. The paper
also never merges two-part ideas into one exhibit: the cost-benefit argument is drawn as
Figures 11 and 12 side by side rather than as panels (a) and (b), which costs a caption
line and doubles the count.

**Table type against data scale.** The lesson is that the WORD and the OBJECT are
independent. Eleven of the twenty-three "figures" are ruled numeric matrices (2, 3, 6, 10,
17-23) and the two objects actually called "Table" are the two smallest grids in the
document (10x2 symbols, 2x6 values). So the reuse rule is not "does this paper use
booktabs" but "which dressing follows which noun": the two real tables are booktabs - top
rule, header rule, bottom rule, no verticals, no zebra - and the eleven figure-captioned
grids are Excel exports with a full box, thin verticals and merged tiered headers. Dressing
here tracks the tool the author pasted from, not the data. Two further patterns are worth
carrying: a grid that grows a column set grows a MERGED GROUP HEADER ("Land Use Type"
spanning six columns, "Ecological Service Value" spanning four), and a grid produced by the
setter pads every cell with a trailing full stop ("599.29.", "0.40621."), so decimal
alignment is perfect and the right edges are ragged - the opposite failure mode from #169,
where hand-typed cells broke the alignment but not the edges. Units migrate as the grid
gets bigger: into the caption for the 2x6 table, into a stub row for Figure 10
("Ecological Value per Unit Area (yuan/hm^2 a)"), and into a second header line only once
(Figure 19's "Million Yuan").

**Caption template.** `Figure <n>: <label>` / `Table <n>: <label>`, colon, no terminal
period (except Figure 1's stray "1."), five to fourteen words, and NOT ONE caption carries
a finite verb about its own object - all twenty-five are noun phrases or stubs. That is a
deliberate-looking house style and it is cheap to copy: the caption names the object, the
prose does the arguing. Position splits cleanly by noun: both table captions print ABOVE
the grid, all twenty-three figure captions print BELOW the art - the #167 pattern, which
now looks like a lane-level convention rather than one paper's habit. Copy the split;
guard against the three ways this paper's caption text breaks: truncated at the page
margin (Figure 10 ends "...in Huangguossh"), split inside a word (Figure 9's "E-" /
"cosystems"), and two captions colliding on one shared line so the left one hyphenates
across the page (Figures 13 and 14, "in Hei-" / "longjiang"). Any of the three forces the
row to be typed from the crop instead of quoted from the text layer.

**Context-linking sentence template.** The positive pattern is a purpose clause with the
verb of plotting in it: "Compare our calculation results with authoritative data to get a
scatter plot in figure 4", "Using the ratio of the various ecosystem ESV in 2015 and 2010,
the radar map is drawn in figure 9", "the ecological value equivalents ... are obtained.(see
in figure 10)". Each names the exhibit AND what the reader is about to do with it, which is
why the reading sentence can follow immediately and state a comparison rather than a
number ("the indirect value and the existence value of environmental degradation are
reduced at a faster rate"). One sentence can also introduce two exhibits at once - "As
shows in figure 11 and figure 12:" - and the bullets that follow then split the reading
between them. The failure to avoid is scale: only 8 of 25 exhibits are ever numbered, the
appendix's seven are never named, and the rest are reached by "The figure shows that..." or
"shown in the appendix", which on a 28-page printout is not a pointer at all.
