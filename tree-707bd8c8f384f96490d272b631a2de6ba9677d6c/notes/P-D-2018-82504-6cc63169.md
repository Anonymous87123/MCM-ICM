## Templates this paper hands you

**Which section takes which exhibit.** The English half is nearly exhibit-free: sections
3.1 to 3.4 carry one line chart, one point cloud, a notation table and a distribution
table, and everything else in those sections is pasted maths. The pictures all arrive in
the results half (4.1 and 4.3), which is where the paper does its showing: a verification
plot, a radius-density curve, two city plans, two growth curves and a province bar chart,
in that order. The reusable rule is the pairing - a model section ends with a table that
freezes its parameters, and the results section then spends one chart per claim. The part
not to copy: the model sections' figures were replaced by screenshots of their own
equations, so a reader who wants the derivation has to read pixels.

**When three dimensions appear.** Never. Forty-three pages, sixteen exhibits, and not one
3-D chart, one contour and no colourbar. The paper's quantitative repertoire is a line, a
bar and a pin map, which is what a policy-and-forecast D paper actually needs: state-level
radius, growth over time, count by province, dots on a basemap. The lesson is negative but
useful - a 3-D surface is a modelling-paper habit, not a forecasting one, and its absence
here is not a shortfall.

**Table type against data scale.** Four of the five grids are three rules and nothing
else - top, header, bottom - and the shape follows the data: a twenty-symbol notation
list becomes 21x2 and overflows the measure by 32 pt, a five-row distribution becomes 5x3,
a three-value radius list becomes 4x2, a four-period forecast turns sideways into 3x5 with
the periods as the header row, and the country classification becomes 4x4. Only the
classification is boxed, and it is boxed because it needs merged cells on two axes - the
GDP band across two columns and the density band across three rows. Worth copying: the
transposed table for time series, and rules only where the grid needs a header boundary.
Not worth copying: the two grids that run past the right margin.

**Caption template.** `<Label> <number>: <noun phrase>` with a space after the label and a
colon after the number - "Figure 3: Verification of the fitting results", "Table 5:
Electric vehicle num in different period" - three to fifteen words, one sentence, no verb,
no unit, no condition, no conclusion, printed below for figures and above for tables. Two
of the eight figure rows carry TWO captions in one printed line ("Figure 5: Plan by Markov
method Figure 6: Plan by SFCLM method"), and in both cases one pasted image holds both
number's artwork - so the welded line is a symptom of a two-panel paste, not of a sloppy
caption. The failure worth avoiding: three captions were left on pages of their own
(p10, p25, p29), so the reader turns a page to find the caption and another to find out
what it was about.

**Context-weld template.** There is no numeric cross-reference anywhere in the paper - not
one "Figure N" in thirty-eight pages of body text. The joins are unlabelled deictics:
"The picture below is the distribution of destination charging stations in Saudi Arabia,
China and Australia.", "As in the pictures shown below, the distribution is similar to
each other, verifying our model's reliability.", "The electric vehicle density by province
in South Korea in different period is as the chart shown." The habit worth taking: the
deictic carries the CLAIM ("similar to each other", "much denser along coastal areas"), so
a reader gets the reading without turning the page. The habit to avoid: nothing in the
sentence says which of two side-by-side plans is Markov and which is SFCLM, and nothing in
the artwork does either.
