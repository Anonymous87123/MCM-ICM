## Templates this paper hands you

**Which section takes which exhibit.** One schematic per idea being defined, one plot per
number being produced, one grid only where the numbers must be read positionally: the
introduction takes the framework diagram, Section 4 takes the ESS wheel and then the gauge
chart that turns the index into a category, Section 5 takes the dual-axis combo that ranks
the projects, the logistic fit and the two panel pairs, Section 6 takes the forecast curves
and the grids they generate, Section 7 takes the two sensitivity sweeps alone, and the
appendix takes the same grid three more times. What to copy: the gauge chart. Figure 3 is a
bar chart whose bars are shaded by distance between two dashed thresholds, so one frame
carries five indices AND their classification, and the prose then quotes the five threshold
pairs rather than the five values. What to copy with care: the six schematics and icon
montages that surround the numbers - Figure 10 restates in clip art what its caption already
says in one line, and nothing in it can be read off.

**When three dimensions appear.** Once, and only for a partition: four exploded 3-D pies
whose slices are shares of 100% (29/71, 33/67, 9/91, 46/54). Every trend in the paper is a
flat line; every schematic is flat. The rule this paper hands you: the third dimension is
acceptable only where the quantity is a share of a whole and the extrusion is decoration -
never on a time series, where it would put a perspective cue on the one dimension the reader
must compare.

**Table shape against data size.** Four columns by four rows of words is a roster (Table 3,
booktabs, no verticals). Two columns by four rows of fitted equations is a list (Table 5,
booktabs). Five columns by sixteen rows with group weights merged across three children and
column fills replacing a second header row is a hierarchy (Table 2, fully boxed, decimal
aligned). Nine columns by five rows whose cells are arrow glyphs plus the words 'Highest',
'Intersection', 'Zero' is a curve written as a grid (Table 4, fully boxed, merged stub) - and
the paper prints that last one four times, once as Table 4 and three times as the appendices,
changing only the stub and the year columns. What to copy: rule density tracks how much the
reader must compare cells positionally, and a template grid is how an appendix is multiplied
without a second design.

**Caption templates.** Two shapes, chosen by position rather than by taste. Above a grid,
no colon and no full stop: "Table N <noun phrase naming the object>" ("Table 2 Weight values
of the indicators"). Below a plot, a full stop after the number and no colon: "Figure.N <noun
phrase naming quantity, units, series>." ("Figure.11 Benefit-cost ratio curve of four
projects which influenced by sustainable development measures only"). Never a sentence,
never a result, never a unit, never a symbol - twenty of twenty captions in this paper are
verbless noun phrases, and the two schematics get four-word captions because the drawing is
self-describing. The pair of panel figures is where the template breaks: naming every series
in the caption costs 42 and 68 words.

**Context-linking templates.** Definition exhibits open the sentence that defines them ("The
whole modeling process can be shown as follows:"); result exhibits are pointed at from the
end of the method sentence that produced them ("... fit it with Gaussian method, such as
Figure.5 shows."); the read-back is exactly one sentence and restates the trend, never the
numbers ("As can be seen from Figure.6, the benefit-cost ratio of the four projects will
increase first and then ..."); sensitivity exhibits are announced by parameter ("we calculate
their real benefit-cost ratio, as shown in Figure 12") and read back as a monotone statement
about that parameter. What to avoid: Table 3 is printed on one page and first named on the
next, the paper's only negative lead, and Table 1 is printed and never named at all.
