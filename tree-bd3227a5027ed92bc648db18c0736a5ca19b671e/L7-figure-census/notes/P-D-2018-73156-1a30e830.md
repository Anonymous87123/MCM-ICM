## Templates this paper hands you

**Which section takes which exhibit.** The paper opens a division with its map and never
returns to it: 3.1 draws the US density map (Figure 1) and the connectivity map (Figure 2)
before any model exists, and 3.2.2 closes the first model with a second map (Figure 3)
whose shading is the model's own output. The optimisation section 3.3.2 then stops drawing
and prints a two-column grid (Table 1) the moment the prose needs four exact integers. The
second country repeats the pattern with one map (Figure 4) and then a single answers
section, 4.5, that carries six exhibits in a row - scatter, two sketches, investment curve,
S-curve and a timeline grid - each introduced by a separate lettered question. A ranking
section (5.2) puts its input grid first (Table 3) and its output dendrogram second
(Figure 10); the sensitivity section (7) is one grid and nothing else; the appendix is
grids followed by the code that produced them.

**When three dimensions appear.** Never. Ten figures and not one lifts the plot: the four
cartographic exhibits are flat projections, the four line drawings are 2-D, the scatter is
orthographic and the dendrogram is orthogonal. Where a third variable has to be shown the
paper writes it ON the flat artwork - the Early/Mid-term/Late annotations inside the
investment curve, the F(x)/G(x) labels beside the sketched curves - which is the template
worth stealing: annotate in place rather than add an axis.

**Table type against data scale.** Two columns and five rows: a four-way allocation with a
single header row, three horizontals, no vertical rule (Table 1). Ten columns and two
rows: a nine-step timeline, and at that width the paper gives up on booktabs and boxes the
grid completely with all eleven verticals (Table 2). Five columns and four rows: eight
countries split into two stacked header-less pairs, horizontals plus interior verticals
only (Table 3). Six columns and four rows: a two-parameter sweep, boxed, and the only grid
in the paper that shades rows, because the shading separates inputs from outputs
(Table 4). Five and six columns with nine-to-four rows: statistical printouts, and they
are the only grids that get a merged title row over a real column-header row (Tables 5, 6).

**Caption template.** Number, colon, lower-case noun phrase, no verb, no unit, no
condition, no conclusion: "Figure 6: Lag index", "Table 4: Sensitivity analysis". Figures
get the caption below, tables above, without a single exception in the paper - and the
unit that the caption refuses to state is pushed into the axis label ("Investment",
"Time"), the table cell (the per-cent degrees) or the prose ("Wherein the blue area is the
densely populated area."). The shortest captions go to the most important exhibits, which
is the opposite of the instinct to describe the hero figure at length.

**Context-weld template.** Lead with a sentence that ends in a colon or a full stop and
names the subject, not the artwork: "The dividing results are as follows.", "The
information is shown as below.", "Their general location is shown as following figure.",
"The curve is exponential." Then the exhibit. Then, four times out of eighteen, one reading
("From Figure 3, we can clearly see that the convenient area has a wide coverage ...") -
and where the reading does appear it starts with the exhibit's name in caps, never with
"as we can see from the above figure". The paper names an exhibit by number exactly twice,
both on the page that prints it, which is the discipline to copy: deixis by default, a
number only when the reader must find the thing again.
