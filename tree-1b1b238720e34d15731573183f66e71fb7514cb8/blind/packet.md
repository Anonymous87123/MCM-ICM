In this paper we build a discrete event model of a space debris field in which every tracked object carries a mass, an altitude band and a cross section. The field grows by collision, and the growth is discrete: one fragment count becomes two, and the new count changes the rate. First, we resolve every conjunction as a pass, a fragment or a merge, and we draw the resolution from a fixed seed so that the run repeats exactly. The model runs a 40 year horizon over 1,200 tracked objects in three altitude bands and reports the object count and the collision rate at five year steps. Under the nominal launch rate the count rises from 1,200 to 2,870 and the rate rises from 0.4 to 3.1 events per year. Second, we rank 24 candidate removal missions by objects retired per unit cost, and the ranking is stable to a 20 percent error in the cost model. Removing the 15 largest derelicts at year zero holds the count to 2,240 and the rate to 1.9, which is the largest single effect we find. A launch moratorium alone holds the count to 2,610, and the two measures together reach 2,050. The top four missions all target the same band, so a programme should buy one band and not spread across three. A programme that buys the top four retires 61 objects for the price of 44 under a spread. A budget half the size of the one we assume buys two of the top four missions and retires 34 objects, which is more than half of the total. The first missions are therefore the efficient ones, and a small programme is not wasting its money. Third, we test the sensitivity of the count to the fragment yield per collision, and a 50 percent rise in that yield moves the 40 year count by 31 percent. This is the largest single uncertainty in the model, and no removal mission addresses it. A programme that ignores the yield plans against a number it does not control. Our model treats fragments as spheres and ignores attitude, so the cross sections are upper limits and the collision rate is biased high. We publish the bias with the result, because a correction needs a shape distribution that our data does not give. The five year step is coarser than the shortest conjunction interval in the data, and a finer step moves the count by 2 percent and the rate by 6 percent. Finally, we publish the seed, the object list and the cost model, so that the ranking can be recomputed for a different programme budget. We also report the count under a removal rate that falls by a third after year ten, and the final count moves by less than a tenth. The ranking of missions does not change under that rate. In conclusion, removal of the largest derelicts gives the largest return per unit of cost, and the fragment yield remains the uncertainty that no mission can address.

## Item 3

In this summary we test whether the price move that follows a news headline is larger than the move that would have happened anyway. We take 84,000 headline and price pairs over four years and label each price change by its size and by the sentiment of the headline before it. First, we compare the observed change with a matched control drawn from the same stock and the same hour on days without a headline, which removes the market-wide move. The excess change is 0.31 percent in the first 30 minutes and 0.44 percent by the close, so the effect persists and does not reverse. Large headlines, which we define as those in the top decile of our sentiment score, give an excess of 0.92 percent, and the bottom decile gives 0.11 percent. The relation between sentiment and return is therefore monotone and not linear. Second, we fit a piecewise model with a break at that percentile, and the fit improves the residual standard error from 1.24 to 1.09 percent. A linear fit misses the flattening, so we accept the cost of one parameter. Third, we test whether the effect survives a trading delay. The signal after five minutes still returns 0.19 percent before costs, and 0.04 percent after a 15 basis point cost, which sits inside the noise of the estimate. We report the strategy as unprofitable at every horizon we test, because the confidence interval crosses zero in all of them. We also split the sample by market capitalisation, and the excess is 0.51 percent for the smallest tercile against 0.22 percent for the largest. The effect is concentrated where trading is expensive, and that split explains most of the apparent profit in small stocks. We check the sentiment score against a hand-labelled sample of 900 headlines, and the agreement is 0.81 in exact labels. The score misses negation, and the two largest errors in that sample both invert a sentence. A negation rule raises the agreement to 0.86, and we use the rule in every result reported here. Finally, we test the matched control on a placebo date one week before each headline, and the excess falls to 0.03 percent. That placebo result is the strongest evidence we have that the headline and not the calendar drives the return. The data set excludes overnight moves, so our estimates apply to the trading day only. This exclusion removes the largest single-day moves in the sample, and an adjustment would rest on an assumption about after-hours liquidity that our data cannot test. A reader who applies these numbers to an overnight strategy should expect a larger effect and a wider error. In conclusion, the effect of a headline is real, monotone in sentiment and too small to trade after costs.

In this paper we treat a sled dog team as a network of animals that must pull in the same direction, and we take the assignment of dogs to positions as the decision that sets the speed. We model the team as a directed graph in which each edge carries a compatibility score estimated from 1,800 recorded runs. The score uses four features: the prior co-running of the pair, their weight ratio, their age difference and the position they occupied. First, we assign dogs to positions by solving a maximum weight matching on that graph. The matching raises the modelled speed by 4.7 percent over the team the kennel actually ran, and by 9.2 percent over a random assignment of the same dogs. Second, we ask how robust the assignment is to a missing dog, and we recompute with each dog removed in turn. The median loss is 1.8 percent and the worst case is 6.4 percent, which identifies three dogs whose absence breaks the team structure. Those three are not the three fastest, so speed and structural importance are different quantities. A kennel that selects on speed alone loses the dogs that matter. Third, we test a greedy assignment against the matching, and the greedy rule loses 3.1 percent of the modelled gain. Greedy is acceptable when a kennel needs a decision in an hour and unacceptable when it plans a season. A fourth test removes the prior co-running feature, and the modelled gain falls by half, so that feature carries the signal. A kennel with no records should expect a smaller gain than we report. We also check the graph against the kennel's own ranking of its dogs: the two agree on the top six and differ on two of the next eight. The disagreement is the part of the score that the kennel does not already know, and it may also be the part the kennel would reject. Our scores come from a single kennel and a single season, and the transfer to another team is not tested here. Finally, we publish the score matrix so that a kennel can re-estimate it on its own records. The paper gives the re-estimation procedure as four steps, and a kennel with one season of records can run it without our code. A team that runs the matching for a full season should expect the modelled gain to shrink as the other teams adapt, and the size of that shrinkage is outside the model. In conclusion, maximum weight matching on four features raises the modelled speed by 4.7 percent, and the structural dogs it protects are not the fastest ones.

In this paper we build a mass and carbon balance for a regional food system in which production, processing, transport and waste are usually counted in different units. In conclusion, waste is the largest and cheapest lever in this region and the ranking is stable under every test we run. A county that weights jobs above carbon should take the second ranking.

We note as well that depriving an ecosystem of all available water carries serious environmental consequences. In our case study, we apply our model to India, which in addition to having the second largest population in the world, also suffers from a large scale lack of safe drinking water and a high rate of waterborne diseases. Taking these two models together, we conclude that the excess water per capita in India will be around half of its current level by 2031. In this paper we model a filled bath as one lumped water body that loses heat to the air, the wall and the bather. We aim to determine when a bather should enter. First, we fit the three coefficients of the model to a published cooling curve without assuming them. The fitted values are 0.9, 2.4 and 1.7 in watts per square metre per kelvin, and each carries a range. The air term dominates when the water is hot and the wall term dominates once the water has cooled, so material matters late in a soak. Second, we compute the entry time that maximises the minutes spent inside a comfort band of 38 to 41 C. For a 200 litre tub with a 5 mm acrylic wall the band opens at 6.4 minutes and closes at 31.8 minutes. The window is therefore 25.4 minutes. Filling the same tub to 300 litres widens the window to 33.1 minutes, a gain of 31 percent for 50 percent more water. The marginal return falls with every further litre. That gain comes from the surface-to-volume ratio, which falls faster than the stored heat rises. Doubling the wall thickness moves the window by 2.7 minutes, while the same change of volume moves it by 8.6 minutes. A bather who wants a longer soak therefore buys volume and not insulation. We also test the common advice to add cold water at the end, and it shortens the soak by 4.2 minutes on average. It never extends the soak in any tub we simulate, although a bather may still prefer the colder water late in the evening. Leaving the tap running cools the water, and it costs 201 litres for 9.1 minutes at the end. Our model fits three published curves with a largest residual under a degree at minute two. The model holds air temperature and bather activity fixed and does not resolve the vertical stratification of a still bath, so it returns one water temperature and not a profile. A rectangular tub behaves like a round one of the same depth, because the model sees surface area and not shape. We expect a shape change to buy under a minute, which is smaller than the volume effect. A bather with a smaller tub should expect a shorter window in proportion to the shortfall in volume; none of the 40 tubs we simulate is smaller than 150 litres. Finally, we publish the coefficients with their fitted ranges so that another tub can be substituted without refitting. In conclusion, the model gives a bather a rule that needs a thermometer and a clock: volume sets the length of the soak and insulation sets only its late part.

We present two mathematical models to assist in the investigation of a serial killer. The models take the locations of previous crime sites and the order that they occurred as variables. These data are few compared to the seemingly infinite possibilities of the criminal's actions, but at least one thing is known: the criminal will do his best to avoid being caught. We infer then, in accordance with past research, that he will kill where he knows the surroundings. This motivates the assumption that the killer is more likely to kill closer to his home or places he has killed before. We further assume that the future locations of attacks depend only on the location of the killer's home and the location and relative time of past crimes. The first of the two models, the "centrographic" model, builds upon earlier work by LeBeau. It uses an analogy to kinematics to derive the location and movement of the "centroid" of a series of crime sites. We consider the change in the location of the centroid over time to predict future crime site locations. In other words, we model the series to have a "velocity" and "momentum" and use this information to predict the future location of the centroid. Our second model, the Rational Serial Criminal model, uses methods from microeconomic theory to model the criminal as a rational agent. This model formalizes the process of looking at a series of crime locations and assessing where the criminal prefers to find victims or dump bodies. Using data on a series of crimes, the model outputs contoured surfaces representing areas where the killer is more or less likely to live. From here, we extend the model to predict where a killer is likely to strike next. We run simulations on crime site data for different serial killers and compare our location predictions to the actual next kill in the series. The centrographic model performs well as measured by the error distance. Un- fortunately, the model is sensitive to a parameter that must be determined for each individual series. The rational choice model also performs well predicting home location as measured by the Hit Score %, a metric which compares the efficiency of a search informed by prediction to a random search.

In this summary we ask what a national statistical office could publish within its existing survey cycle, given the staff and the instruments it already has in place. A green gross domestic product is a policy instrument, and an instrument that no ministry can compute is not one. Firstly, we build an index that uses four published series and one new survey item, and we avoid a full natural capital account of the country. The index reweights consumption by a depreciation term for three national stocks over a twenty year window: forest, soil and fossil reserves. Secondly, we apply the index to 14 countries over 20 years and compare the result with the published green domestic product where one exists for those countries. Our index tracks that published series with a correlation of 0.88 and a mean absolute deviation of 3.1 index points. That deviation sits inside the revision band those series already carry, which is the test we set ourselves before running the comparison. We ask which of the four series does the work, and the forest term alone reproduces 0.71 of the correlation. A statistical office with one survey to spare should therefore spend it on forest cover and not on soil, which is the cheapest version of the recommendation. We cost the survey at 0.4 percent of the existing statistical budget and the annual compilation at two staff months of effort. We test the index against three alternative weightings, and the published comparison holds within 0.06 of the correlation in every case. The result does not rest on our weighting choice. A country that omits the fossil term altogether still reaches 0.79, and that is the cheapest version of the index we would defend in public. We test what happens when a country's national forest survey is five years old, and the correlation falls to 0.62 from 0.88. That fall is larger than the gain from adding soil, so survey frequency matters more than survey breadth. The policy reading is that a country can publish a defensible green product in two years, and the binding constraint is survey capacity and not method. It omits water entirely, because the water accounts we could obtain were not comparable across the 14 countries in the sample. Second, a country whose fossil reserves are revised by more than 10 percent a year sees the depreciation term dominate the index. In conclusion, the forest term alone gives a defensible green product within the existing survey cycle, and the full index can follow as the accounts improve.
