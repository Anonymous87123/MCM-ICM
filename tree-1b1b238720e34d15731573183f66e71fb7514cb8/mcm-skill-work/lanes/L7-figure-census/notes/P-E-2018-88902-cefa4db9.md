## Templates this paper hands you

**Which section takes which exhibit.** The split is by epistemic job and it holds for
all eighteen exhibits without a single crossover: Introduction and Model get diagrams
(six figures across 1.2, 4.1, 4.2, 4.3.1, 4.3.4, 4.3.5), and Analysis onward gets
computed numbers (seven plots across 6.2, 6.3, 7.1, 8, 9.1, 9.2, 11, and all five
tables). The sub-pattern worth copying is that a section that DEFINES something gets a
drawing and a section that MEASURES something gets a plot - 4.3.4 gets the Markov
network the moment the factorisation is asserted, 4.3.5 gets the three-slice DAG the
moment the conditional dependency is asserted - and no section gets both. What to copy:
one diagram at the point of definition, one plot inside the paragraph that produces the
number, and a case-study section that gets exactly one figure per subsection (9.1 the
radar and the year table, 9.2 the prediction line and the prediction table). What not
to copy: the strategies table. Table 5 is written for 10 Task 4 and printed on p22
under the heading of 11 Task 5, so the exhibit lands after the section that supersedes
it; that failure mode is invisible if you only check that the caption and the grid sit
next to each other.

**When three dimensions appear.** Exactly once, Figure 9, and never as a surface: four
perspective scatter boxes in a 2x2 grid, one per three-way combination of the four
indicators, drawn so that the paper can claim it showed all four dimensions at once.
The condition that triggers it is a dimensionality argument made out loud in the prose -
"Because we have four dimensions, which can't be shown in only one figure" - and the
resolution is four 3-D boxes with three named axes, five divisions each and NO tick
numbers, plus three colour-coded clusters with no legend. Copy nothing about the
execution; the point to copy is the admission. A paper that says in the text why one
flat figure cannot carry the result has done the reader a service, and this one throws
that good faith away with an unlabelled scale. The other twelve figures are flat, and
several are flat pictures of three-dimensional claims (the WFSI wheel's quadrant
gradients, the 2-slice network's time axis), which is the cheaper way out.

**Table type against data scale.** Three grades and the boundary is what the reader is
meant to compare. A summary of metrics (Table 1, four columns) and a pair-by-pair
comparison (Table 2, eleven columns) both get the heavy treatment - closed frame, full
internal grid, lavender label band over header and stub - because the reader is meant to
scan rows against each other and the grid lines are the reading aid. A year-by-year
record (Tables 3 and 4, six and seven columns, five rows each) gets booktabs: three
horizontal rules, no verticals, no shading, because there is nothing to compare across
columns, only down one. The intervention plan (Table 5, four columns, eight rows) gets
the paper's only zebra striping, and that is exactly the case where alternating stripes
earn their keep: eight rows of prose-length cells where the eye must not slip into the
neighbouring row. Two rules to carry forward. First, size tracks named objects, not
numbers - eleven columns because there are four node pairs, seven because there are five
years, eight rows because there are six interventions plus a total - so a table's shape
is fixed by the argument before any data arrives. Second, the paper's own two table
failures are copy-paste failures: Table 4's caption says "WFSI C" and silently drops the
CCIC column Table 3 carried, and every cost cell in Table 5 welds number and unit
("$8 million/year") so the column cannot be summed. A column list belongs in the caption,
and a unit belongs in the header.

**Caption template.** `Figure <n>: <noun phrase>` / `Table <n>: <noun phrase>`, colon,
sentence case, four to eleven words, one sentence, no terminal verb, no unit, no
condition, no conclusion - and always BELOW the exhibit, for figures and tables alike.
Inside that frame this paper varies one slot usefully: the object being measured. Eight
of eighteen captions name it ("Figure 10: Impact of CCIC.", "Table 3: WFSI and CCIC for
Greece from 2008 to 2012."), which is the difference between a caption that lets a
skimming reader reconstruct the argument and one that only lets them find the exhibit.
Copy the naming. Do not copy the two defects: "Table 4: WFSI C" truncates a variable
name, and Figure 11's caption has no terminal period and runs straight into the body
sentence, so on the page the caption and the following prose read as one paragraph.

**Context-linking sentence template.** Two moves, and the paper alternates them by
habit. For a defined object the pointer is a plain `direct` sentence placed before the
art ("We learned the following Markov network from data, representing the pairwise
relationships between the five indicators.") or a `parenthetical` glued to the claim
("the original and post-evidence results are shown in the same graph(Figure 10) for the
purpose of comparison."). For a computed object the pointer is `as-shown-in`, and it
almost always carries the scale or the caveat in the same breath - "we set the radar
chart boundary to 0.35 to better show the trend of the changes", "as shown in Figure
13(The gray area means that we have no data of it)". That is the single most copyable
habit in the paper: the sentence that points also tells the reader how to read. The
matching tail sentence then states the reading in one clause and stops - "The data above
suggests that, although the current status of Greece is acceptable", "We can then draw
the conclusion from Table 4 that Greece may turn to fragile state in 2016". What not to
copy: the teaser paragraph. Figure 7 prints on p16 under a sentence that names no
figure, and the paper does not number it until p17, which is the paper's only negative
lead_pages and the only reason a reader has to flip back.
