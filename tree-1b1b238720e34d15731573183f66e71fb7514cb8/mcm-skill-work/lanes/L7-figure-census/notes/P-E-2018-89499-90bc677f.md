## Templates this paper hands you

**Which section takes which exhibit.** The paper allocates by stage of argument and never
doubles back: the notation section takes the symbol table (2.2), the model-construction
section takes all five flowcharts (3.2.1 for the three layer hierarchies, 3.2.2 for the
coupling system), the model-test section takes the first computed chart and the first
result table (3.5.1 Figure 6, 3.5.2 Table 2), and from there every section takes exactly
the exhibits its own paragraph produces - Sudan gets a fragility chart, a variance chart,
a relationship table and a rank table across 4.1.1, 4.1.2 and 4.2; Egypt gets the same
four across 5.1.1, 5.1.2 and 5.2; the intervention plan gets the cost sheet at 6.2 and the
before/after chart at 6.3. What to copy: one exhibit per claim, placed inside the section
that makes the claim, with the mirror sections of the two case studies receiving
structurally identical exhibits. What not to copy: the mirror discipline applied to the
PROSE. The Egypt section inherits the Sudan section's sentences wholesale, so 5.1.2 is
titled "How Climate change impacts on fragile Sudan", Figure 9's lead sentence still says
"we determine the fragility of Sudan", and Table 5's caption is Table 3's caption with a
different number. Copy the structural symmetry; rewrite the sentences.

**When three dimensions appear.** Never. This is the first paper in the lane with twelve
figures and not one of them is multipanel, three-dimensional, colourbar-bearing or
sharing an axis - every exhibit is a single flat panel with a legend below it or no
legend at all. The dimensionality problem is solved instead by adding a SECOND y axis:
Figure 6 puts rank (20 to 140) on the left and fragility (0 to 0.7) on the right so that
two incommensurable quantities share one x axis of thirty countries. That is the whole of
the paper's visual sophistication, and it is worth copying precisely because it is
cheap: two scales on one frame beat a 3-D box every time, and the reader is not asked to
estimate depth. The cost is that the two series are read against different grids, so any
figure that does this must label both axes, which Figure 6 does ("Rank" implied by tick
values, "Value" printed) and Figure 7 does not (one "Value" axis carrying both the stacked
segments and the fragility line).

**Table type against data scale.** Two dressings, chosen by whether the reader compares
across columns or only down one. A symbol list (Table 1, 2 columns) and a band
classification (Table 2, 2 columns) get a single rule under the header and no bottom rule
- the lightest possible grid, because there is nothing to scan across. Everything with
three or more columns gets a closed frame with a full inner grid (Tables 3, 4, 5, 6),
because the reader is meant to run down the measures and back across the rows. Two rules
carry forward. First, alignment follows the data and not the paper: Table 3 aligns four
decimal places on the point and Table 5, which carries the identical measures for the
identical five climatic variables, does not, because one of its columns prints 0.04545
next to 0.0223 - so a census that records "this paper aligns decimals" is wrong, and the
field has to be recorded per grid. Second, a unit belongs in the header, and this paper
puts it in the cells: every cost in Table 6 welds its currency ("50,000,000 dollars") and
the flowchart of Figure 2 pushes units into the feature boxes ("% of GNI"). A cost column
with welded units cannot be summed, which is exactly the check a reviewer runs first.

**Caption template.** `Figure <n> <noun phrase>` and `Table <n> <noun phrase>`, no colon,
no terminal period, sentence case, five to twelve words, one sentence, no unit, no
condition, no conclusion - and the position flips by exhibit type inside one document:
all six table captions print ABOVE the grid, all twelve figure captions print BELOW the
art. That split is the single most copyable structural fact here, because it means the
reader's eye is trained by this paper to look up for a grid and down for a chart, and a
document that puts both below it (as #168 does) forces a re-scan on every switch. Inside
the frame one slot is worth varying: whether the caption names the quantity. Five of the
seventeen recorded captions do ("Figure 7 Fragility of Sudan", "Figure 10 Variance of
Stress, Status and Response Indicators in Egypt") and three name a layer instead ("The
Theme Layer and Feature Layer of Stress Indicators"), which tells a skimmer what the
exhibit measures rather than merely which one it is. Do not copy the welding: two printed
captions run their number into the first word ("Table 2Range of three levels", "Table
5Relationship Index ..."), and Table 1's caption exists only as ink - the text layer skips
from "2.2 Notations" to "3 The Model of Fragility" with no caption line and no table at
all.

**Context-linking sentence template.** One formula carries twelve of the seventeen
pointers: "<subject> as shown in Figure N", with the subject doing the argument's work
before the reader reaches the exhibit - "the structure of both theme layer and feature
layer is shown in Figure 3", "the stress indicators, the status indicators, the response
indicators are shown in Figure 8". The three direct pointers are reserved for computed
objects the paragraph has just produced ("we determine the fragility of Sudan and the
level from 2012 to 2016 as follows", "listed in Table 6 below"), and the two exhibits with
no pointer are instructive rather than accidental: Figure 6 prints before its own reading
sentence, and Table 1 is never announced. The matching tail sentence is always one clause
with one number in it - "from 2012 to 2015, the change of fragility mainly comes from the
change of response indicators as 57.39%", "the total cost makes up for almost 3.365% of
the GDP in Egypt" - so the pattern is: pointer states the claim, exhibit shows the shape,
tail states the magnitude. Copy that three-beat rhythm; do not copy the copy-paste that
leaves "the fragility of Sudan" inside the Egypt section.
