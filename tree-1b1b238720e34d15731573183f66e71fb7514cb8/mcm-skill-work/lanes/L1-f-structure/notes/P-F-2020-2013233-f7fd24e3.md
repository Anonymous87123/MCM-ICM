## introduction — 1 Introduction (1.1 Background, 1.2 Statement of the problem) (pp. 3-4, 13 blocks)

`route → ext:context → ext:context → ext:context → ext:context → route → source → route → route → route → route → route → route`

Four background paragraphs, all context and none quantified: migration has never stopped and ocean temperatures keep rising (14); climate migration creates humanitarian crises and regional conflict and is recognised as a 21st-century challenge (15); environmental displacement aggravates existing socio-economic contradictions (16); the field is not new but international cooperation is still weak (17). Block 18 states the client and the remit - the ICM-F asks for a model advising the UN on resettlement and cultural-heritage protection and on the policy's impact - and it is the only place the paper says who the decision-maker is, which is why the whole paper is an advisory rather than an optimisation. Blocks 19-20 quote the UN ruling that states cannot return people whose lives are threatened by climate change, with the passage on a country becoming submerged, then name Tuvalu, Maldives and 'Kisbass' (Kiribati) as future refugee states - the quote is the only legal source in the chapter. Blocks 22-26 restate the five tasks in one paragraph each, and the restatement is looser than the problem: Task 1 is narrowed to 'six countries' and 'number and development trend', Task 3 mixes human-rights protection with cultural exchange, Task 5 is 'determine the dynamic effects the model ignores and modify our model' - a task about fixing the model rather than about the policy's importance. Block 26's tail carries the '2 General Assumptions / 2.1 Assumptions' heading.

## assumptions — 2 General Assumptions and Justifications (2.1 Assumptions) (pp. 4-5, 10 blocks)

`route → limit → limit → limit → limit → limit → limit → limit → limit → limit`

Nine assumptions, one sentence each, and the section title promises justifications that are never given. (1) A country whose sea level is below five metres counts as a source of climate refugees - the trigger for the entire forecast; (2) the six selected countries are representative, so the cultural-loss mechanism transfers - the assumption that licenses generalising from six states; (3) migration is in groups, one origin to one receiver, though a receiver may take several groups; (4) refugees do not choose their country, UNHCR allocates; (5) receivers must accept allocations but may refuse at capacity; (6) no terrorism or disaster follows the arrival; (7) refugees get the same legal status as residents; (8) no ethnic isolation, so cultural exchange always occurs; (9) international-organisation policy is compulsory and perfectly implemented. Assumption 9 is the one that makes the policy simulation meaningful and it is also the least plausible - it removes the political resistance that the paper's own background chapter says is the central obstacle. Assumption 1's five-metre threshold is the same as the World Bank below-five-metre population data used in Task 1, so the definition and the exposure measure coincide by construction. Block 36's tail carries the '2.2 Variable Description' heading.

## results — 3 Task 1 (3.1 forecast of refugee numbers, 3.2 possible cultural loss) (pp. 6-7, 10 blocks)

`source → select → route → read-figure → compute → route → select → read-figure → derive → interpret`

The forecast is thin: six island states are chosen (Kiribati, Nauru, Marshall Islands, Bahamas, Maldives, Tuvalu) on the strength of 'related reports', Table 2 lists each country's population, share of land below five metres, terrain height, annual sea-level growth and whether the population lives below five metres, and a regression on 'sea level data collected in previous years' returns about 4 mm/year, from which the paper concludes that by the end of the century many territories will be uninhabitable. The regression itself is never shown - no functional form, no coefficients, no data window - and the 4 mm/a figure is a slope that could be read off a chart. The headline number (200,000-300,000 refugees by 2080) appears only in the summary and the importance chapter, not here; here the chapter stops at 'they must have residents move out'. The cultural-loss half selects six risk factors (nationalist tendency, geographic location, fit of production and lifestyle, carriers required by culture, refugee cohesion, economic benefit of culture) into a 6x6 matrix of e_ij values (Table 3), then aggregates by principal components with weights y_i (block 75) and reads three qualitative properties - deeper cultural dependence lowers risk, stronger exclusion raises it, more complex carriers raise it (block 76). No principal-component loadings, no variance explained, and the matrix entries are not sourced. Block 76's tail carries the '4 Task 2' heading for the best destination.

## model-establishment — 4 Task 2: optimal flow of climate refugees (bipartite graph, AHP weights, KM matching) (pp. 7-9, 14 blocks)

`route → define → define → define → define → source → derive → derive → read-figure → compute → compute → derive → read-figure → route`

The matching model: r = a1 r1 + a2 r2 + a3 r3, where a is the weight vector, r1 is receiver acceptance = mean education level (1-6) times per-capita GDP, r2 is refugee adaptation measured by the stock ratio S1/S of earlier migrants from the same origin, and r3 is the natural factor, taken as distance because 'the longer the distance, the more difficult'. Each sub-model is verbal and has one number attached: r1's example value is 2.8, and r3 enters as 0.0327 in the worked example. The weights come from an AHP-style reciprocal matrix on r1/r2/r3 (1, 3, 4; 1/3, 1, 2; 1/4, 1/2, 1), giving a = (0.57, 0.34, 0.08) - no consistency ratio is reported, unlike the AHP in the other 2020 papers, and 0.08 for distance is small enough that geography barely matters. Block 83 adds environmental responsibility measured by consumption-based rather than territorial CO2, citing the Paris Agreement - a good methodological choice (it charges importers for embodied emissions) that is stated but never enters the matching formula, since only three weights exist. The worked match gives 2.0416 for A to B, the graph is then matched by the KM algorithm, and the chapter ends without reporting the resulting flow: no country pair, no share, no table. The model is therefore fully specified but unexecuted, and the reader never learns where any of the six countries' refugees would go.

## model-establishment — 5 Task 3, 5.1 Analysis of human rights protection (5.1.1 theory, 5.1.2 model, 5.1.3 analysis) (pp. 9-12, 20 blocks)

`route → route → source → select → define → define → define → define → route → define → derive → derive → derive → derive → compute → define → define → derive → define → route`

Human rights are operationalised as five per-capita indicators - education, medical expenditure, grain output, pension expenditure, housing area - each justified by one sentence tying it to a right (the right to education, to health, to life, to welfare, to shelter), then combined by PCA into an index C = 0.269 z1 + 0.054 z2 + 0.257 z3 + 0.279 z4 + 0.309 z5 (block 114). The PCA is described properly (orthogonal transformation, greatest variance on the first component, components uncorrelated) but the loadings are given without the data matrix, the standardisation, or the share of variance explained; and the coefficients are suspiciously even (0.269, 0.054, 0.257, 0.279, 0.309), which suggests a normalised first eigenvector of roughly equal weights rather than a data-driven one. The dilution mechanism is the chapter's real idea: per-capita indicators fall when refugees arrive, because the denominator becomes P + P(t) (blocks 115-119, worked on grain output), so protection capacity is a decreasing function of intake - which is exactly what makes resettlement a trade-off with the host population. Block 120 notes the political consequence (resource diversion causes local dissatisfaction and unrest) and thereby links this chapter to the game-theoretic pressure idea in the other 2020 papers, though this paper does not model the conflict.

## model-establishment — 5.2 Cultural exchange and dissemination (5.2.1 culture types, 5.2.2 model, 5.3.3 analysis) (pp. 12-14, 17 blocks)

`route → source → define → source → define → route → derive → define → derive → define → define → derive → define → derive → define → define → interpret`

Culture is typed into three categories, and the typology is the paper's best idea: income culture (art and intangible heritage), which is assumed to add value to the receiver's economy; loss culture (religion and customs), which is assumed to generate conflict and economic loss, supported by the Crusades and the Iran-Iraq war (block 124); and neutral or swing culture (lifestyle - food, clothing, housing, transport), whose sign depends on policy tolerance. The income case is a growth formula in which country A's cultural GDP is multiplied by (1 + n1) and scaled by X, the policy impact on marketisation (blocks 127-128). The loss case defines K, the likelihood of riots, from a standard loss rate BS times d, where d = f(w)^3 is a decreasing function of tolerance, and the actual loss rate SS = BS times d (blocks 129-134) - a cubic chosen for shape, with the tolerance variable w never mapped to a policy instrument. The neutral case turns on b, the policy tolerance for neutral culture: high tolerance commercialises the lifestyle and makes it income-like, discrimination converts it into a loss culture (blocks 135-136). Every step is a sign claim with a chosen functional form and no estimated parameter, and the three types are then simulated. The analysis block (137) reports only that country B's gains and losses balance early and diverge later, and that a multi-ethnic state 'will re...' - the sentence is cut in the extraction - so the result is qualitative.

## recommendation — 6.1 Policy recommendations (human rights: a-g; culture: a-f) (pp. 14-16, 36 blocks)

`route → handoff → ext:claim → handoff → handoff → handoff → handoff → handoff → handoff → handoff → handoff → handoff → handoff → handoff → handoff → handoff → handoff → handoff → handoff → handoff → handoff → handoff → handoff → mechanism → handoff → handoff → handoff → handoff → handoff → handoff → handoff → handoff → handoff → ext:context → handoff → handoff`

Two lists of recommendations, one per task. Human rights (a-g): recognise refugee status without racial or religious discrimination; choose an agricultural or non-agricultural resettlement method depending on land availability; build infrastructure and guarantee food, shelter and medical allocation, with subsidies; promote integration through government, society and refugee effort, including legal aid, job search, counselling and cultural activities; invest in refugee education, including teacher conditions, school access, hotlines and language-plus-values curricula; open employment through labour-management dialogue, job information and training, cutting wage deductions and document retention; and target support by household type. Culture (a-f): resettle in groups so that communities retain cohesion; protect cultural heritage, on the argument that the real carriers of culture are people rather than organisations; strengthen refugees' identity with their own culture by recording intangible heritage and oral history; marketise income culture through cultural industries, museums and tourism; minimise the losses from religion and custom; and convert swing culture into income culture by respecting it and commercialising food and clothing. The list is extensive, concrete and mostly about implementation rather than about this paper's models: only (a) group resettlement, (d) marketisation and (f) swing-culture conversion follow from the Task 3 typology, and none of the recommendations carries a cost, a target or an indicator. Block 165 is the one mechanism claim (culture is inherited through people, so people are the unit of protection).

## results — 6.2 Policy impact (system-dynamics simulation) and 6.3 optimisation (pp. 16-18, 16 blocks)

`route → define → define → define → compute → read-figure → interpret → interpret → interpret → interpret → interpret → interpret → interpret → route → define → compute`

The policy is simulated by changing the per-capita indicators: food aid raises the numerator (Z3 + delta Z3) while the denominator keeps the refugee population (blocks 179-182), and employment adds two new indicators, the number of employed refugees z6 and tax revenue z7, so the index becomes C(t) = 0.269 z1 + 0.054 z2 + 0.257 z3 + 0.279 z4 + 0.309 z5 + f(z6, z7) (blocks 183-185). The simulated path (Figure 3) is read as four stages: UN resource input raises capacity for everyone; education and employment let refugees create value that offsets the cost of the inputs; later refugees integrate and pay taxes; and high tolerance reduces the riot probability so the loss culture's drag shrinks (blocks 187-191). The cultural reading repeats the same four stages for identity, marketisation, rights recognition and religion (blocks 192-194). Then the model is corrected: population is not constant, so the denominator becomes (P + P(t)) times (m1 - m2), and the index is re-simulated as Figure 4, which is said to be 'more in line with the actual situation' (blocks 195-198). No axis values, no units, no numerical output from either figure are reported - both are read qualitatively - and the policy lever enters only as an assertion that parameters move in a favourable direction. The one structural point is the birth-minus-death correction, which is a real improvement to the dilution model but arrives with no data on either rate.

## strengths-limitations — 7 Policy importance, 8.1 Strength, 8.2 Weakness (pp. 19-20, 10 blocks)

`interpret → ext:claim → ext:claim → ext:claim → ext:claim → limit → limit → limit → limit → limit`

Block 199 is the importance argument: about 200,000-300,000 homeless by 2080, the true number is larger because of the method's limits, and 326,600 is Iceland's 2014 population - the comparison is meant to make the figure vivid but the two numbers are not commensurable (Iceland's whole population against a global flow). Four strengths: consumption-based emissions are used for responsibility, which avoids the unfairness of trade-adjusted accounting; culture is split into three types, which makes an unquantifiable problem tractable; nationalism is considered; and the long-run dynamic impact is modelled with new factors (blocks 200-203). Four weaknesses plus a stray fifth: only six countries, so the forecast scope is narrow; the index system rests on representative factors and may be biased; NGOs other than the modelled ones matter; non-marketised cultural existence is ignored; and 'lack of dynamic analysis from a nationalist perspective' (block 208), which sits outside the numbered list and is the most substantive of the five, since nationalism is one of the six cultural-loss factors and is never given a dynamic. References follow block 212.

## conclusion — 8.3 Future work (pp. 20-21, 4 blocks)

`route → route → route → route`

Four directions, each a task for someone else: add temperature-rise and ecosystem indicators (coral reefs and marine fish protect land) to the refugee forecast; clarify what 'land disappearance' means, since the trigger could be hurricane and tsunami exposure, total submergence, or salinisation that kills crops - the sharpest self-criticism in the paper, because the five-metre rule is exactly that unresolved definition; model cultural assimilation explicitly rather than assuming no early impact; and analyse nationalism dynamically. Block 212 carries the fourth item and the 'Work cited' heading, so the paper ends with future work and has no conclusion chapter at all - the five tasks' results are never gathered into a single answer.
