## memo — Memorandum to the Director of LIFE (policy recommendations) (pp. 2-3, 7 blocks)

`route → route → ext:claim → select → select → select → limit`

A memo, not a letter: date, addressee, subject line, then seven blocks of policy. The structure is unusual for this corpus - it states three properties of the policies (scalable at 10000 per 26 months, representative across models, durable to 2100) before it states any policy, so the memo advertises robustness that the body never tests at that scale. Three named policies follow: Gov-Corp as sole firm, a lifelong-learning requirement with three production options, and equal male/female representation by occupational sector, each with the reasoning attached. The last block is the only limiting one ('our recommendations are sensitive to the composition of Population Zero, but not as much to the size') and the table of contents starts inside it.

## introduction — A. Harnessing Complexity: 10 Year Vision (pp. 4-5, 3 blocks)

`ext:context → ext:context → route`

The paper opens its body with a vision essay, not with a task list. Block 20 says the colony is an opportunity to redesign incentive structures; blocks 21-22 import the Greek polis and propose the government-firm ('Gov-Corps') that the whole paper then assumes; block 23 defines complexity as the modelling frame (many variables, interdependencies, stochastic plus deterministic interplay) and names the three networks studied. The polis material is doing argumentative work - it justifies a single employer - but it is presented as historical inspiration with one citation, and measurement split it at the page break.

## notation — B. Defining Utopia: Metrics of Success and Key Parameters (pp. 5-6, 4 blocks)

`define → define → define → define`

Four definition blocks that fix the paper's measurement vocabulary: Gini for income, two retention factors for gender (take-up of full leave, exit after multiple children), lifelong learning for education, and human capital H as the cross-cutting stock. The chapter cites the sociologists and economists it borrows from, and the H definition is the one that later carries the education model. Measurement split two of the four at page breaks (26/27 and the heading 24 detached from its body), so the paragraph count on the page is roughly twice the group count.

## model-establishment — C. Population Zero: Demographics for Sustainability (pp. 6-8, 9 blocks)

`route → source → define → define → read-figure → define → derive → define → read-figure`

Population Zero is built by selective Monte Carlo, and the paper is explicit about why (variability for robustness, control for ideal distributions). The data split is stated: income from the Census, education from an ideal distribution (10% PhD, 30% MS, 40% BS, 20% AS), gender and ethnicity set equal by fiat. Human capital is operationalised as thresholds (30 units at ten years old, 70 at AS graduation) and read off Figure 1. The age distribution is the only genuinely derived piece: sustainable death rates from an 80-year life expectancy, then a 5-65 window imposed by the four-month flight and the distribution recomputed rather than truncated. That recomputation is the reason the later 100-year runs oscillate, a link the paper makes itself two chapters later.

## assumptions — D. Assumptions behind the income model (pp. 8-9, 6 blocks)

`route → limit → limit → limit → limit → read-figure`

Four numbered assumptions, each measured as three or four line-fragments because the bullet text wraps. They are unusually load-bearing: wages equal marginal output, agents are rational and never borrow, minors do not work, and the Gov-Corp is the only employer. The first and fourth together remove the labour market from the model - there is no unemployment, no competition and no outside wage - which is what makes the inverse wage curve in the next section a policy instrument rather than an equilibrium. The caption 'Figure 3. Income Distribution and Policy Implementation' sits between the assumptions and the model and is the only visual anchor for the flow the text describes.

## model-establishment — D. Addressing Income Inequality (pp. 9-11, 11 blocks)

`define → source → derive → define → source → source → define → derive → define → derive → compute`

The income model is built from borrowed numbers, and the paper says where each comes from: Norway for the labour-force rate (71.2%, rounded to 70%, giving 5600 workers), a 27000-person Connecticut sample for the wage curve, a Norwegian union wage of $15/hour for the minimum, $20000 per pupil for education, $2407 per capita for government. The wage curve is fitted in Excel and then rescaled (W(x) = 62205676/(5670-x) - 970), which is why it has a pole at x = 5670 just past the top rank. Taxation is the chapter's output: a progressive scheme, then a capped version at 50% after the first fit produced an implausible top rate, and the effect is measured only by Gini (0.58 pre-tax to 0.36 post-tax). The 50% cap later becomes the reason a mass migration cannot be absorbed, so this block is the hinge of the whole paper.

## model-establishment — E. Education: Living and Learning on Mars (pp. 11-13, 6 blocks)

`route → derive → compute → define → derive → read-figure`

Two education models, explicitly separated: a weighted decision matrix for curriculum, and a discrete dynamical system for human capital. The matrix is built on Wikipedia's discipline list and hand-set outcome weights (30% values, 70% economic application), so its ranking is an authored judgement dressed as a computation - the top disciplines come out as Systems Engineering, Computer Science and Chemical Engineering. The human capital model is the only place where rates are estimated ('1 additional unit per year of work and 5 per year of learning', from 'experimentation'), producing 9/19/24 units for the three production options. Note the figure numbering slip: the text announces Figure 5 and the caption printed below is Figure 4, and the age-distribution figures later appear as 5 and 6.

## model-establishment — F. Social Equity: An Economic Model (pp. 13-15, 10 blocks)

`route → define → limit → define → define → define → define → compute → interpret → select`

The equity chapter is the paper's most concrete model: maternity and paternity leave are equalised into parental leave, justified by two borrowed rates (human capital depreciates 1% per month on leave, accrues 0.2% on the job), and the couple's exit decision is written as an explicit inequality between childcare cost and opportunity cost. Two cost formulas are then defined symbol by symbol across four blocks. The 5000-couple Monte Carlo produces the sensitivity ordering the paper acts on (childcare cost first, then fertility, then minimum wage), and the chapter converts that into the one priced recommendation of the paper: $7500 per child, yielding 5-6% of the population as childcare providers. The claim that gender ratios come out equal is circular - the simulation was given equal male and female wage distributions by assumption.

## model-establishment — G. Modeling Subgroups (pp. 15-17, 8 blocks)

`route → define → interpret → interpret → interpret → interpret → interpret → interpret+close:compare`

Six subgroups from a two-way split (three salary bands by child status), then one interpretive block per subgroup. There is no subgroup model here: each block narrates what that group would want and which policy answers it, with no simulation and no metric attached, so the chapter is the paper's weakest evidence and its most readable prose. The closing block is a genuine comparison - the poor value welfare and education instrumentally, the rich value education intrinsically, the childless want mobility - and it states the redistribution trade-off (benefits for the disadvantaged at the cost of high tax rates) more plainly than the recommendations do.

## results — H. In the Long Run: 100 Year Vision (pp. 17-21, 10 blocks)

`interpret → interpret → read-figure → interpret → compute → interpret → select → read-figure → interpret → read-figure`

The 100-year chapter reports four trends and revises policy in three of them. Population is sustained but oscillates, and the paper traces the oscillation to its own 5-65 truncation of the age distribution. Gender equality degrades because the Earth pipeline cannot supply enough women in mechanical engineering (277500 jobs against 32727 degrees, 7.9% of the occupation), which the model turns into a handicapped female wage distribution and roughly double the childcare-provider exit rate for women. The poleis then interact like firms, and the proposed fix is citizen migration from old to new poleis. Curriculum is re-weighted toward innovation and cohesion, giving a different top six. Human capital falls when Population Zero dies and recovers through the LLL programme, with rising dispersion that the paper interprets as beneficial rather than as a problem. Three of the four results are read off figures (5, 6, 8, 9) with no table.

## results — I. External Shock: Earth Evacuation (pp. 21-23, 7 blocks)

`route → derive → derive → define → derive → interpret → interpret+close:limit`

The shock chapter argues a negative result analytically instead of simulating it, which makes it the sharpest reasoning in the paper. Four equation blocks set up the government budget identity (G = T), write spending as population times cost per head and revenue as tax rate times output, then break output into A, L, K, H, N. The inequality follows: maintenance costs scale with n, output does not, because technology, capital and land cannot double when migrants arrive. The verdict is that the only two instruments (raise the tax rate, cut spending per head) both destroy the utopia, and the 50% cap set earlier in the income model is the binding constraint. One sentence of the same block extends the argument to education funding.

## strengths-limitations — J. Conclusions (pp. 23-24, 4 blocks)

`ext:claim → limit → limit → select+close:handoff`

Four blocks: strengths, two weaknesses, and a summary with future research. The strengths are sector-by-sector optimisation and compatibility with larger populations - both claimed, neither shown, and the second is contradicted by the shock chapter that precedes it. The first weakness is the honest one: there is no unifying variable, since income uses wages, equity uses human capital and curriculum uses judged needs, so the three models are not one model. The second weakness is the data base (American sample, Nordic targets, no real utopia to calibrate against). The closing block proposes a unified human-capital model as future work, and the reference list starts inside it.
