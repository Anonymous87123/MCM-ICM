## assumptions — 2 Assumptions of models (pp. 3-4, 5 blocks)

`limit → limit → limit → limit → limit`

Five numbered assumptions, each two to five printed lines. (1) Population density decreases linearly with elevation, justified by the claim that people live on the plain because mountains are unfit for farming and fishing. (2) A country's EDPs migrate to one country as a whole, because their number is only tens of thousands and a receiving country can absorb a whole country; the block is truncated mid-sentence ('And it's better to'). (3) Accepting EDPs or donating aid is economically harmful to the receiver once jobs, living space, welfare and local discontent are counted - the assumption that makes the game non-trivial, and it is asserted without evidence. (4) Policy is influenced by pressure from other countries, which may sanction a non-contributor. (5) EDPs take the new nationality and equal rights, with negotiation for those unwilling; the UN only guarantees an option. Assumption 3 and 4 together are what justify modelling resettlement as a game, so the whole of section 5 rests on two unevidenced claims.

## model-establishment — 4.1 The way of simulating an island country (4.1.1 geography, 4.1.2 population distribution) (pp. 4-6, 14 blocks)

`route → route → route → route → read-figure → derive → route → route → source → define → derive → read-figure → derive → define`

Geography is a right circular cone: the country is described by two observables, average altitude H and territory area S, and everything else is inferred by elementary geometry - eq (1) gives R (base radius) and L (slant length) from H and S, and A = R/L, the sine of the cone's slope. The derivation is correct but it assumes every country is a cone whose average altitude is its peak height, which for a country like the Maldives (average 1.8 m, no interior) is a very strong simplification; the paper never checks the resulting R against a known coastline length. Population: uniform density is rejected because people cluster in cities, so a World Bank dataset of the population living below five metres is used to correct a linear density profile rho = k l (eq 2), with k solved from P = integral of 2 pi l rho dl = 3 P A/(2 A pi L^3) (eq 3; the extraction of the constant is garbled) and then re-solved with the below-five-metre count P_p and the depth D (eq 4). Note that eq (3) as printed integrates over the whole cone volume, so the density is per unit volume, not per unit area - the model treats a cone as a stack of shells, which is why the exponent on L is 3. Blocks 75/76 are the sub-heading split from its text, and 81/82 split the same way.

## model-establishment — 5.1 Countries in responsibility, 5.2 The basic attitude towards EDPs (pp. 9-11, 24 blocks)

Responsibility: three factors - cumulative carbon emissions since the eighteenth century (direct responsibility), total GDP (international morality and justice), population density (capacity to absorb) - combined linearly as R = 0.5 CB* + 0.3 GDP* + 0.2 PD* (eq 8), all normalised to maximum type. The weights are set by hand and the only defence is the ordering of the factors, so the ranking in Tables 5-6 is a scale chosen by the authors: the US at 0.991 is nearly four times Canada at 0.276, which follows almost entirely from the carbon term. The eight top countries become Group A (US, China, Japan, Russia, Canada, France, Brazil, Australia) and the next twelve Group B. Two coding choices matter: population density enters as a benefit (a dense country is a weaker candidate) while carbon and GDP enter as duties, so the index mixes capacity and responsibility in one number without saying how the two are traded. 5.2 then handles the legal question: a state without territory cannot be called a state, so if a humane country donates land the EDPs are not refugees; otherwise they are refugees and must be treated fairly (block 142). The three stated attitudes (blocks 144-149): the international community offers a last resort; the last resort guarantees rights but not nationhood, all EDPs become citizens of the new country and are accepted as a whole to protect culture; and all countries provide economic support. Block 149's tail carries the '5.3 Game of great power' heading.

## model-establishment — 5.3 Game of great power (5.3.1 pressure simulation, 5.3.2 game rules) (pp. 11-13, 20 blocks)

`route → define → define → define → route → define → define → define → mechanism → route → define → define → define → define → define → define → define → define → route → read-figure`

The eight Group-A countries are the players; each chooses x_i = (accept?, donate?) in {0,1}^2 with the interest ordering (0,0) > (0,1) > (1,0) > (1,1), so a country prefers to do nothing and the game must be driven by pressure (eqs 9-11). The solution space is 2^16 = 65,536 states (block 154 says '48 kinds', which is wrong: 8 countries times 4 strategies is 32 one-shot profiles, and the paper's own end condition allows many combinations), so a simulation is used instead of solving. Pressure: P_t = [p_ij] with the pressure on j equal to the column sum, and the most-pressured country moves one step along its priority sequence (blocks 157-159). The rules that give p_ij values are: higher ranked country bears more pressure (block 161-163); stronger countries project more, capped at 8.0 (blocks 164-168); and the exact value comes from a cubic in the difference between the country's expected outcome XE and the actual X, with sign-dependent coefficients fh+ and fh- capturing political favour and hostility (blocks 169-179). The cubic is chosen so that 'bigger difference means much bigger pressure' with no scale argument, the 8.0 cap is arbitrary, and the paper concedes at block 181 that the expectation vectors and the favour/hate coefficients 'couldn't be a quantitative factor' - so the model's two most important inputs are judgement calls. What remains is a coherent negotiation protocol, and the chapter is honest about being a simulation of politics rather than a solution.

## model-establishment — 6 The protection and inheritance of culture (6.1 change of people and culture) (pp. 15-16, 14 blocks)

`route → define → ext:context → route → define → define → define → limit → define → read-figure → define → derive → derive → route`

Three inheritance routes are named (transmission within the nation, acceptance by others, written record), then two of them are set aside as remedies and the model is built on the first (blocks 200-201). The population is split into A (locals and assimilated immigrants with little knowledge of the immigrant culture), I (immigrants who fully master it) and M (mixed descent and amateurs who partly master and spread it). An explicit size argument justifies a shortcut: a receiver usually has over 10 million people and takes no more than 100,000 refugees, so A is assumed far larger than M or I for a long time (block 207) - this is what makes the marriage probabilities tractable. Children's category is a probabilistic function of the parents' categories, given in Table 9 for six family types (AA, AM, AI, MM, MI, II) with a parameter alpha in [0,1] 'dependent by nation relationship and policy' (blocks 208-211). The ODE system in eq (12) then tracks M and I with birth rate kb, death rate kd approximately equal, a constant probability p that an M or I marries an A, and random marriage otherwise. Note the table and the equations use different labels: the table writes A/M/I, the text of block 208 says 'A×A, A×B, A×C, B×B, B×C, C×C', and block 206 is one printed line. The model's logic is clear: alpha is the cultural transmission advantage of the immigrant group, p is the exposure to locals, and policy is meant to act on both.

## model-establishment — 6.2 The solution to the model (6.2.1 numerical cases, 6.2.2 Lyapunov analysis) (pp. 16-18, 14 blocks)

`route → route → route → read-figure → interpret → route → derive → derive → derive → derive → derive → derive → derive → interpret`

Two numerical cases, then a stability proof. Case 1 (kb = 0.0155, kd = 0.015, p = 0.9, alpha = 0.4): the culture declines and is almost lost after 700 years, the 'complete integration' case (block 219). Case 2 (p = 0.05, alpha = 0.8): an initial decline then convergence to a stable positive level, the 'own society with limited contact' case (block 221). Note the figure panels are mislabelled - the left panel is headed (a) Situation 2 and the right (b) Situation 1 - so the readings in blocks 219 and 221 refer to captions that disagree with the panel labels; the parameter values and the described shapes are consistent with the intended order, and the extraction cannot settle it. The analytic part: eq (12) has a single interior equilibrium (M*, I*) given in block 223; the Lyapunov function is quadratic, f = a(M-M*)^2 + b(I-I*)^2 + c(M-M*)(I-I*), and its derivative along the flow is bounded as in eq (13); with a = b = 1 and c = 2 kb p /(kb p alpha - 2 kd) the quadratic form is negative semidefinite when p < kd/kb and alpha >= 2 kd/(kb p) - 1, and the discriminant condition (eq 14) gives f > 0, so the equilibrium is stable and the solution converges to a positive point (blocks 224-232). The algebra is a standard Lyapunov argument and it is the most rigorous passage in the paper. The interpretation at block 233 is the policy lever: keep p small and alpha large, i.e. limit contact with locals and strengthen transmission of the immigrant culture. That is a real trade-off, and the paper states it without softening it.

## model-establishment — 6.3 Politics design guided by the model (pp. 18-19, 15 blocks)

Five policies are listed against the two model parameters: strong culture protection (raises alpha), local education (raises the child's cultural transmission, i.e. alpha), encouragement of non-government communication (raises p), inhabit policy (concentrating the EDPs, raises alpha and lowers p) and scatter policy (raises p). Table 10 is referenced for the p and alpha changes but appears in the extraction only as the tail of block 246 (a five-level 'Bad ... Good' scale); Table 11 is the policy combination table (blocks 245-247). The reading at block 246 is the paper's most honest paragraph: no policy is all-powerful, protecting culture has a price, concentrating EDPs invites accusations of discrimination, and assimilating them arouses nationalism. The recommendation is a sequence: first inhabit plus local education for a few decades while the two groups adjust, then strong protection plus scatter once relations are good (blocks 249-254). The sequencing follows from the model only loosely - the model says p low and alpha high is stable, and the recommended sequence raises p (education, communication) before lowering it, which the paper does not reconcile; the argument is that nationalism must be defused first, a political constraint the model does not contain.

## sensitivity-analysis — 8 Sensitivity analysis (8.1 cone accuracy, 8.2 responsibility weights, 8.3 culture parameters) (pp. 20-22, 14 blocks)

`route → route → derive → check → limit → route → read-figure → check → route → route → read-figure → interpret → read-figure → interpret`

Three tests, each honest about what it cannot show. The cone: dS/dv0 = pi L^2 (2H - 2v0 - ay)/H^2 and d2S/dv0^2 = -2 pi L^2/H^2 (eq 15), so sink area responds less than proportionally and the second derivative is negative - the model is 'well robust at the beginning of the year' and, more importantly, block 267 states the validity range: early times, small rise, hundreds of years but not thousands. That is the only place the paper restricts its own forecast horizon, and it is more informative than the derivative. The responsibility weights: w_carbon is varied and Figure 7 shows R and the ranking barely move, so the eight Group-A countries are stable (blocks 268-270) - but the test holds w_GDP and w_PD fixed, so it does not test the trade-off between duty and capacity, only the carbon weight. The culture model: fixing alpha = 0.4 and moving p, the convergence rate and point are insensitive for p near 1 and sensitive for p near 0; fixing p = 0.05 and moving alpha, both are insensitive (blocks 272-276). The asymmetry is real and matters for policy - the model is most sensitive exactly where the paper's recommendation (p small) sits.

## strengths-limitations — 9 Strengths and weaknesses (9.1 strengths, 9.2 weaknesses) (pp. 22-23, 6 blocks)

`route → ext:claim → ext:claim → ext:claim → route → limit`

Three strengths and one weakness paragraph. Strengths: close to reality, because density is modelled and the 30% trigger conforms to practice, and because game theory reflects how states negotiate (blocks 278-281); practical culture protection, because measuring culture by the number of speakers makes protection quantifiable and lets policy move p and alpha (blocks 282-284); and responsibility plus ability, because the index uses emissions, density and GDP (blocks 285-289). The first strength is the one to question: the 30% trigger is a choice, not a calibration, and the paper offers no evidence that states act at 30%. The weakness is stated in one breath and is the accurate self-assessment: the game cannot predict any country's future action, and the policy cannot predict how much it shifts the marriage and inheritance probabilities, 'which make it difficult to specify our policy' (block 291). That concedes the central gap - the model gives directions (lower p, raise alpha) but no magnitudes, so the policy chapter's timetable ('a few decades') is unsupported. References follow block 291.
