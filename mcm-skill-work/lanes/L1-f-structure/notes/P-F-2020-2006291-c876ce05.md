## model-establishment — 3.1 Model I-1: Forecasting the number of climate refugees (3.1.1 problem analysis) (pp. 4-5, 7 blocks)

`route → route → route → define → read-figure → limit → route`

The section heading is measured as three blocks (28 '3. Model Design', 29 '3.1 Model I-1: Forecasting', 30 'Number of Climate Refugees By Time 3.1.1 Problem analysis') and coded as one heading group. The analysis is a two-step design: regress sea level on global mean temperature and a time trend, then translate a predicted sea-level rise into flooded area and affected population. Block 33 makes the geometry assumption that decides everything downstream - the territory is a flat circle and the intrusion is linear at 35 cm of sea-level rise per kilometre of land (cited to [2]) - and this is the only place the conversion factor appears. Block 35 adds the population assumption: nearly half the world lives within 150 km of a coast [3], assumed to be exactly 50% evenly spread in that band and the rest evenly spread elsewhere. Block 36 restates the procedure. Both assumptions are stated as facts with a citation but neither is tested, and the 50% figure is a rounded reading of 'nearly half', which the paper then treats as exact.

## model-establishment — 3.1.2 Statement of the model (regression, planar erosion, affected population) (pp. 5-6, 10 blocks)

`define → define → derive → derive → source → derive → define → derive → derive → define`

Two regressions and one geometry. Sea level: SL = c + b1 temp + b2 temp^2 + b3 t + mu (eq 1), then a first difference to remove non-stationarity, giving the fitted difference equation dSL = 8.826 - 2.086 dtemp + 0.07 dtemp^2 + 0.003 t (block 42). No standard errors, t-statistics, R^2 or sample period are reported anywhere, and the sign on dtemp is negative while the paper's whole narrative is that temperature raises sea level - the fit implies a warmer year lowers sea level, and the negative linear term is 'corrected' only by the positive quadratic. Land: the territory is a circle of radius R, the sea erodes a ring of radius r with r = beta * SL, so S_subm = pi(R^2 - r^2) (blocks 45-50, the middle of which is garbled by the equation font). Population: with a the offshore population share, P = P_world * a * S_subm/S_offshore for the one-ring case and a two-term expression when the ring passes the 150 km band (block 52). Blocks 37/38 and 45/46/47 are single sentences cut by the inline equations, and blocks 53-60 are the symbol list one fragment per block ('Where,', 'S_offshore', 'refers to the land area offshore ...'), which is why nine groups cover 24 blocks. Every symbol in the population model (S_offshore, S_world, P_world, a, r_offshore) is defined here and nowhere else.

## model-establishment — 3.2 Model I-2: Culture loss evaluation model (pp. 6-7, 7 blocks)

`route → source → define → handoff → define → derive → define`

Culture is valued as the sum of three components: V = w_h * heritage + w_l * landscape + w_c * custom (eq at block 63), with weights 'weighted by the comprehensive moving average of the years, comprehensive economic income, and radiation range' - a description that names no formula, so the weights are free parameters. Heritage value is to be looked up in the World Heritage List and in World Bank valuations of official organisations (block 65), and the same block lists the preservation methods (3D modelling with VR for material heritage, other means for intangible) - a plan, not a measurement. Landscape value is V_lands = sum AVE_i * I_i over the landscape's lifetime, where AVE is annual valuation and I the economic index (blocks 66-68), with a duration set and a sum over the lifetime; the paper notes the user may discount by inflation or expected return. Customs and dialects are declared 'difficult to estimate ... to quantified cultural value, so we introduce weights' (blocks 68-69). The model therefore has three free weights, an unspecified duration, and a heritage term that is a look-up - which means the 22.462 billion USD loss computed in the Maldives case is not reproducible from the paper. Block 70 carries the '4. Model II: Immigration Decision System / 4.1 Problem Analysis' heading in its tail.

## model-establishment — 4.1 Problem analysis and the six-submodel architecture (pp. 7-8, 7 blocks)

The architecture chapter: an intermediary human-rights organisation matches refugees to similar natural and geographic environments, consults the receiving country, and collects fees from the countries with carbon obligations to sponsor the receivers (blocks 71-74, which is one paragraph split three ways around the right-hand figure, and whose tail carries the Figure 4 caption text). Blocks 75/76 then announce four sub-models - AHP for suitable countries, an immigration stress level model for capacity, a nonlinear programme for timing, a cost-and-obligation model - and blocks 78-80 add what is in effect a fifth and sixth: the temporary residence model for sudden shocks (block 78 is a 235-word restatement of II-2's purpose and includes the political-stability argument) and the sub-model purposes for II-3 (timing: 'country A will be completely submerged in ten years') and II-4 (compensation plus an emission-reduction incentive based on emissions per unit GDP). Note the numbering is inconsistent across the paper: the summary sheet and block 80 count six sub-models (AHP, ISL, NLP, cost, temporary residence, and the prediction/culture pair), while section 4 lists four; block 80's tail carries the 'Model II-1: AHP' heading. Block 77 is the Figure 5 caption (the AHP hierarchy diagram).

## model-establishment — 4.2 Model II-1: AHP (eight indicators, pairwise matrices, consistency) (pp. 8-11, 21 blocks)

`route → read-figure → define → define → define → read-figure → define → define → define → define → define → read-figure → define → read-figure → define → read-figure → define → read-figure → derive → derive → check`

Eight indicators in three criteria groups (A1 rights and development: B1 HDI; A2 natural environment: B3 temperature, B4 precipitation, B5 location; A3 culture and values: B2 treatment, B6 government form, B7 religion, B8 language). Each indicator gets a scoring rule: B1 = HDI; B2 = 4/3/2 for national treatment, refugee treatment, non-refugee treatment (Table 2); B3 = |DAT_in - DAT_out| on decade-average temperature, with B4 and B5 by analogy (B5 first takes the borderline/coastline ratio of each country, then differences the ratios); B6 ranks republic 3, constitutional monarchy 2, monarchy 1 (Table 3) and takes |R_k - R|, with a 6x6 difference matrix for the three types (block 95); B7 is a 5/3/1 religion-acceptance score (Table 4); B8 is a 4/3/2/1 language-match score (Table 5). Two design problems are visible in the rules themselves: the indicators are differences of levels, so a country with a colder climate scores as badly as a hotter one, and the B5 ratio difference mixes coastline length (a size measure) with location (a proximity measure). The aggregation is the standard AHP eigenvector step, w_jk = sum a*b (blocks 101-102), with a consistency ratio CR = sum CI/sum RI (blocks 103-105), and block 106 states the pass condition. The tables appear in the extraction only as captions and fragments - Table 1 is a garbled two-criterion layout (block 82), and Tables 2-5 are captions plus row fragments - so the score scales are recoverable but the pairwise matrices are in Appendix 1, which is not in the extraction.

## model-establishment — 4.3 Model II-2: Immigration stress level model (ISL) and its threshold (pp. 11-13, 16 blocks)

`route → source → select → route → read-figure → define → read-figure → define → derive → derive → define → route → mechanism → compute → read-figure → interpret`

The paper's most developed behavioural model. Motivation is political: citizens who feel overwhelmed vote for anti-refugee parties, and the examples given are the US withdrawal from the Paris agreement after Trump, and refusals of Chinese and other entrants (block 108, which cites no source for the election claims). Five indicators are selected - GDP growth, unemployment, inflation, crime rate, population growth (block 109, 'Economy is the most significant factor') - standardised to [1,2] (block 118), then turned into pressure terms P = 2*ZNP^2*3.75/4 - 1/3.75 for the positive variables and the reciprocal form for the negative ones (blocks 127-130; the algebra is written with the square of the refugee share in the numerator or denominator, and the constants 3.75, 4/7 are never justified). Stress = sum w_i P_i with weights [2, 1, 2, 0.5, 1] (blocks 131-133), also unargued. The square term is the only piece with a stated theoretical basis: utility theory says marginal utility of gain falls while marginal aversion to loss rises, so disgust rises faster than refugee numbers (blocks 135-136). The threshold is then estimated backwards from politics: the stress level is computed for the US in 2016, Italy in 2018 and Hungary in 2018, on the argument that the anti-immigrant parties won there, so those years must sit at or above the tolerance threshold (block 137). Block 139 reads the computed values - about 13 for the US, about 15 for Hungary and about 18 for Italy - and rounds them to a global threshold. That is circular: the threshold is defined by three elections and then used to predict what other countries will tolerate. Tables 6 and 7 are the variable lists, measured as seven blocks each (caption plus one symbol per block).

## model-establishment — 4.4 Model II-3: Nonlinear programming model for the migration plan (pp. 13-14, 5 blocks)

`derive → define → define → interpret → derive`

The plan is max sum_ij W_ij * mark_ij subject to stress_j,t <= stress_0 for every year (blocks 140-141, where the constraint is written with the stress computed by the ISL model and the welfare W scaled so that the shares sum to one). Blocks 142-143 define the four symbols (T the execution horizon, welfare the cultural and treatment quality index, stress the ISL output under the plan, W_ij the share of country i's EDPs taken by country j). Block 144 reads the model qualitatively: the more urgent the event the more countries are involved, a country with a low Model-I score is a poor cultural fit, and therefore earlier UN action is better. Blocks 145-149 then give the two-step solution - because welfare is monotone in mark, first compute each country's maximum intake N_i,t under the stress cap, then allocate W_ij within those caps - and this argument is split one clause per block by the inline subscripts. No instance of the programme is written out (no objective coefficients, no bounds, no solver), and block 146's 'Pr ess' is the extraction's mangled 'stress'; the claim that the two-step procedure solves the programme exactly is asserted, not proved.

## model-establishment — 4.5 Model II-4: Cost and obligation model (pp. 14-15, 7 blocks)

`route → route → source → derive → define → define → derive`

Total cost = the number of EDPs times per-person resettlement cost times a treatment/identity multiplier, plus the cultural value at risk times the share the UN agrees to compensate (blocks 152-155; the equation itself is unreadable in the extraction because the PDF's math font maps to private-use glyphs, and blocks 156-159 are the four symbol definitions one fragment each). The per-person costs come from 'news reports and previous studies' (block 153) with no citation list entry, and Table 8 ('Rank & Identity') appears in the extraction only as a caption - the cost table itself is missing. The obligation side is the more interesting half: responsibilities are set by historical cumulative emissions, with an incentive coefficient that reduces a country's bill if its emissions per capita fell over the review period, defined as the geometric mean of the year-on-year ratios (blocks 160-163). That is a working mechanism for 'reduce emissions and pay less', but the weighting between the two components (historical share vs incentive) is not stated, and the geometric mean of ratios is not normalised, so the shares need not sum to one - the Maldives case does not report whether they do.

## model-establishment — 4.6 Model III: Temporary residence model (pp. 15-17, 15 blocks)

`route → route → route → define → define → define → define → define → derive → derive → source → derive → derive → define → read-figure`

The short-run counterpart to AHP, built on three dimensions: spatial distance between capitals (shorter better), the population ratio of the two countries (smaller better), and the receiving country's area (larger better) - blocks 166-171, which also show how the first two are inverted so all three point the same way. The scoring model is C = w1*(1/d) + w2*(P/P_h) + w3*S after min-max standardisation to [0,1] (blocks 172-175), with weights (0.4, 0.3, 0.3) taken from a reference [6] rather than elicited - so the trade-off between proximity and capacity is imported. The allocation step is a greedy fill: sort countries by score, add population up to an absorption cap alpha, then split the intake by the weighted average of scores (blocks 178-183), and stop adding to any country once EDPs reach 5% of its population (blocks 184-187). That 5% cap is the only hard political limit and it is asserted with no source, while the ISL model in 4.3 estimates the political tolerance far more elaborately - the paper never reconciles the two. Block 188 carries the algorithm flowchart caption ('Figure 5', which reuses the figure number already used for the AHP diagram) and the '5. Case I: Save Maldives' heading.

## results — 5. Case I: Save Maldives (at stake, AHP, scheme, cost and obligation) (pp. 17-20, 21 blocks)

`source → read-figure → read-figure → source → derive → compute → compute → route → check → interpret → read-figure → route → source → route → interpret → route → interpret → interpret → compute → source → compute`

The one fully worked case. Setting: 1,192 coral islands, 298 km2, 200 inhabited, average elevation 1.8 m (blocks 189-193, with Table 9 and Figure 7 as captions only). Sea-level forecast: at 15.35 C global mean temperature sea level rises 0.67 m by 2035, and at +1 C it reaches 1.44 m by 2045 (block 194, printed '20450') - both numbers are produced by the difference regression of 3.1.2, which had the negative temperature coefficient, so the forecast inherits that problem. Culture loss: heritage 17.524 bn Rufiyaa (weight 0.26), tourism 6.74 bn with a 75-year duration (0.69), GDP 224.7 with lambda 0.43 (0.05), total 349.734 bn Rufiyaa = 22.462 bn USD (block 197) - note the GDP term (224.7) is four orders smaller than the other two, so its 5% weight is what keeps it from vanishing, and the 75-year undiscounted sum is what makes tourism dominate. AHP: consistency ratio 0.0068 with maximum eigenvalue 3.007 (block 199), and Malaysia, India and Indonesia rank first (block 200, weight table in Appendix 3). Plan: scenario I (early action, 7 years) sends only to Malaysia at 70,000-80,000 a year, with a dip in year five when Malaysia enters a simulated recession; scenario II (5-year evacuation) adds India at 40,000-50,000 a year (blocks 206-208). Recommendation: begin 7 years before the islands become uninhabitable (block 209). Cost: 552,147 people at about $6,000 each plus 5% of the 22.462 bn cultural value = $3,425,182,000 (blocks 210-212), then allocated across the G20 by 1990-2016 emissions (blocks 213-214, with the G20 share of world GDP given as 85%). Block 214's tail carries the '6. Case II: Fragile Japan' heading. The Monte Carlo and Phillips-curve machinery described in block 203 is never reported: no distribution, no interval, only the point path.

## results — 6. Case II: Fragile Japan (sudden disaster, temporary residence) (pp. 21-21, 5 blocks)

`source → route → compute → compute → interpret`

The stress test of Model III, and the extraction measures it one printed line per block (18 blocks for five sentences), which is why five groups cover blocks 215-232. Setting: Japan ranks first in the 2020 Global Climate Risk Index and the Fukushima leak is cited as evidence of fragility (blocks 215-219). Scenario: a tsunami or nuclear leak displaces 50% of the population and a temporary country is needed at once; the absorption ratio 1.45% is raised 2.5 times for the emergency (blocks 220-223). Result: ranking the neighbours by the three-dimension score, the top three - Russia, China, South Korea - can absorb the whole safe-haven population (blocks 224-226), and the weighted split is adjusted for countries that hit the 5% cap (blocks 227-229). Closing: after the shock people either return or are resettled through the Case-I system, 'so there is no more details' (blocks 230-232). No numbers are given for the split between the three countries, no score table, and the 1.45% baseline ratio appears nowhere else in the paper - the case demonstrates that the algorithm runs, not what it recommends. This is also the end of the body: the paper has no conclusion, strengths, limitations or sensitivity chapter at all, and the reference list begins at block 233.
