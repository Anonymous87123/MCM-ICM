## introduction — 1 Introduction (1.1 Background, 1.2 Our work) (pp. 3-3, 6 blocks)

`ext:context → ext:context → route → route → route → route`

Three pages of front matter and two paragraphs of background. Block 11 is the only sourced fact in the chapter - 16,344 digital currencies with a total market capitalisation of US$120.2 billion, cited to CoinMarketCap - and it is the paper's single market statistic. Block 12 names the three defects the whole paper is organised around (instability, evasion of supervision, criminal use) and carries the 1.2 heading. Blocks 13-18 are the four work items, each split at a line break, ending with the Figure 1 caption glued to the Assumptions heading. The work items map cleanly onto the paper's three models, which is more than most papers of this type manage: item 1 is the DSGE model, item 2 the PCA factor screen, item 3 the regulatory/KNN chapter, item 4 the spatial autoregressive forecast. What the introduction never does is state the ICM tasks in the problem's own words, so the mapping from Task 1-7 to sections has to be inferred from the work list.

## assumptions — 2 Global assumptions (apply to all models) (pp. 3-4, 4 blocks)

`route → limit → limit → limit`

Three assumptions and one lead-in sentence, and they are stated as scope decisions rather than as modelling devices: no unified world currency is required, no country is required to abandon its physical currency, and digital currency is issued only by the central bank. The third is the strong one - it excludes private cryptocurrencies, which are the subject of the introduction's market statistics and of the whole motivation, so the paper's model describes a system that does not yet exist while its evidence describes one that does. The first two assumptions are also what allow the paper to avoid ever modelling currency competition between sovereign and digital money; contrast the 2019 papers that make that competition the centre of the analysis. Block 22's tail carries the Model 1 heading.

## model-establishment — Model 1: financial-system construction - scope, assumptions, nomenclature (pp. 4-5, 16 blocks)

`route → route → source → limit → limit → limit → limit → limit → limit → limit → limit → limit → limit → limit → limit → limit`

The chapter states its lineage honestly: the model follows Barrdear and Kumhof (2016) and Qian (2018), and the authors explain the one deviation that matters - Qian assumes central-bank digital currency fully replaces cash, whereas this paper keeps a holding ratio so that partial replacement can be studied. That deviation is the source of the paper's only policy variable (the share of households holding digital currency) and it is the right thing to have changed. The thirteen assumptions are then listed, and they are a DSGE closure set rather than digital-currency choices: no foreign deposits or trade, digital and physical money both interest-bearing at the same rate, sticky prices and wages, fixed consumption habits, indistinguishable deposits, rational households, two firm tiers with monopolistic intermediate producers, bank-financed capital, reserve rate equal to the CBDC rate, an interest-rate corridor, no retained bank funds, risk-free-plus-premium pricing, and inflation targeting. Assumption 2 is the one to flag: making digital currency and deposits perfect substitutes in return removes the substitution margin that a CBDC is supposed to move, and the model then has to reintroduce it by hand through the utility term and the holding-ratio constraint. Blocks 26-43 are heavily fragmented (each assumption's wrapped line is a separate measured block); block 44 packs assumption 13 and the entire Table 1 nomenclature (Pt, ct, Dt, Bt, Et, mu, At, gamma, chi, theta_t and the rest) into one block.

## model-establishment — Model 1: the DSGE model (family, firm, commercial bank, central bank, general equilibrium) (pp. 5-8, 15 blocks)

`derive → derive → define → interpret → define → derive → define → define → define → derive → derive → derive → derive → derive → derive`

A complete four-sector New Keynesian model, written out with the standard apparatus and almost no explanation. Household: nominal budget constraint (1), its real version (2), utility u_t = log c_t - phi n_t - (phi_d/2)(d_t - d_{t-1})^2 where the third term is a deposit-adjustment cost, then the CBDC constraint alpha_t c_t <= d_t with alpha_t = 1 - v_t and an AR(1) for the log share of digital-currency holders, driven by the shock q_t. Firm: Dixit-Stiglitz aggregation (8) with elasticity mu, the demand curve (9) and price index (10), Cobb-Douglas production (11) with TFP A_t = exp(chi t + theta_t) and an AR(1) for theta, capital accumulation (14), bank-financed working capital L_t = W_t n_t + P_t i_t, quadratic price and capital adjustment costs (16)-(17), profit (18) and the discounted objective (19). Bank: balance sheet l_t + m_t = d_t + b'_t, profit (22) with reserve rate R^C, corridor rate R^T, loan rate R^L, deposit rate R^D, plus an AR(1) for risk-management ability w_t, the equality R^C = R (24) and the pricing rules R^D = R + r1 and R^L = R + r1 + r2 with AR(1) premia. Central bank: balance sheet (30) and a Taylor-type rule (31) with inflation coefficient phi_pi. General equilibrium: output equals consumption plus investment plus the two adjustment costs plus the two bank costs. The omission is systematic and it is stated: 'the Lagrangian equation is solved to obtain its first-order condition, which will not be explained too much here' - twice, for the household and for the firm, and once more for the bank. So none of the Euler equations, labour supply, price Phillips curve or loan-supply condition is actually displayed, which means the steady state reported in Section 3.4 cannot be checked by the reader and the model's dynamics are asserted rather than derived. Blocks 78-88 are the bank block, and they mix six equations with their symbol glosses, which is why the group spans eleven measured blocks.

## results — Model 1: implementation, steady-state results, shock, sensitivity (pp. 8-9, 10 blocks)

`route → define → define → compute → define → check → interpret → route → read-figure → check`

The results chapter is four moves and it is more careful than the derivation. Parameters are calibrated in Table 2 (beta 0.7, phi 1, alpha 0.5, gamma 0.7, chi 0.02%, delta 2%, w 4, sigma 0.60%, mu 5, rho_v 0.8, rho_w 0.6, rho 0.8, rho_r1 0.8, rho_r2 0.8, phi_P 4, phi_k 0.2, r10 0.40%, r20 2%) - a table of borrowed values with no per-parameter source, and several of them (beta = 0.7, mu = 5, sigma = 0.6%) are far from standard quarterly calibrations. Steady states: at full digital-currency holding (l = 1) the reserve ratio is 17.8% and household consumption is 75% of GDP; at l = 0.5 they are 26% and 68.2%; at l = 0 they are 32.9% and 63%. Block 102 then compares these with the real world - reserves around 15%, final consumption about 80% of GDP in 2017 per the World Bank - and the paper's argument is that l = 1 is closest, so full replacement is feasible and desirable. That is the paper's central claim and it is a three-point comparison against two aggregates, with no standard errors and no statement of how far off is acceptable. The shock exercise sets the CBDC shock's standard deviation to 0.0006 and reports Figure 2 as showing that a positive shock raises consumption and output 'in the long run' - a phrase that is wrong for an impulse response. The sensitivity test is one sentence: every parameter perturbed by 10%, conclusions unchanged.

## results — 4 Key factors: principal component analysis on 14 indicators (pp. 9-11, 19 blocks)

`route → route → route → define → define → define → define → interpret → define → define → define → interpret → define → interpret → define → interpret → define → interpret → interpret`

The PCA chapter answers Task 3 (factors limiting or facilitating growth, access, security, stability) and it is the paper's most empirical section. Fourteen indicators are collected for countries with population over 20 million or GDP over $100bn, grouped into eight per-capita-GDP grades, and PCA reduces them to four components with cumulative variance 0.3996, 0.5505, 0.6679 and 0.8016 - so four components carry 80% of the variance, which is a defensible stopping rule. The loadings in Table 5 are reported in full, and the four interpretations follow the loadings: PC1 (access) loads on inflation, government corruption and credit rating; PC2 (growth) on broad money, GDP and budget; PC3 (stability) on population and unemployment; PC4 (security) on the broad-money-to-reserves ratio and the exchange rate. Two problems are visible in the numbers themselves. The loadings are not clean - population loads 0.346 on PC2 and PC3's largest loading is unemployment at 0.2826 while population is only 0.0476 - so the labels attached to the components are more confident than the loadings warrant. And the sample is 'the same number of countries randomly extracted' from each grade with no statement of how many, so the 14x14 correlation matrix that produces these loadings cannot be reconstructed. The chapter's prose is also the most fragmented in the paper: blocks 117-150 are one line each because the PDF sets them line by line, so nineteen coded groups cover what are visually eight short paragraphs.

## model-establishment — 5.1 Model 2: overall design of the regulatory mechanism (pp. 11-12, 8 blocks)

`ext:context → route → select → select → select → select → select → limit`

The design chapter is a five-part prescription with one honest closing caveat. Risks are enumerated first (speculation, money laundering, platform illegality, resource waste, cited to a Chinese source), then three regulatory architectures are compared - decentralised supervision of every issuer, physical support, and central-bank issuance - and the third is chosen because sovereign states are already exploring it and because issuer supervision solves the accountability problem. The five design elements are: legal status for digital currency backed by central-bank credit, the central bank as regulator (with the argument that it already has research departments and controls issuance), a real-name account system with optional commercial-bank distribution, and a globally unified regulatory model to prevent regulatory arbitrage. Block 159 is the caveat that matters: the system cannot eliminate risks such as money laundering, which is precisely why the next chapter builds a risk classifier. The design is coherent but it is not derived from Model 1 - nothing in the DSGE model requires a real-name system or a global regulator, and the paper never connects the two.

## model-establishment — 5.2 Model 2: KNN classifier on a vector-space model for money-laundering risk (pp. 12-15, 26 blocks)

`route → route → define → define → define → define → define → define → define → define → define → define → define → define → define → define → define → define → define → route → read-figure → read-figure → interpret → interpret → route → interpret`

This is the paper's most concrete and most reproducible model, and also the one with the smallest data footprint. The data are the UK HM Treasury national risk assessment for 2015: twelve thematic areas (banks, accountancy, legal, money service businesses, trust/company providers, estate agents, high-value dealers, retail betting, casinos, cash, new payment methods, digital currencies), each scored on four dimensions - total vulnerabilities, total likelihood, structural risk and risk with mitigation. Each area becomes a point in a four-dimensional vector space, the eleven non-digital-currency areas form the training set, the vectors are projected to 2D for visualisation, and kNN with k = 3 classifies the digital-currency area. The result: digital-currency money laundering in the UK in 2015 is Low risk, the same class as casinos, high-value dealers and retail betting, and the paper's recommendation is that digital-currency supervision can borrow from those three regimes. That inference is the paper's best piece of applied reasoning, because it names the comparators and the reason (similar risk structure). Three limits are visible: the training set is eleven points, so k = 3 is a fifth of the data; the four scores come from a single jurisdiction and year, so the classifier is a restatement of the Treasury's own assessment rather than an independent test; and the chapter never reports an error rate or a held-out validation (the later 'sensitivity to sample size' discussion admits the training set should be expanded). The many consecutive `define` jobs are the definitional core of the chapter - the twelve areas, the four scores, the three factors behind each, Table 6's nomenclature and equations (34)-(36).

## model-establishment — 6 Model 3: spatial autoregressive forecast - assumptions, nomenclature, model (pp. 15-17, 17 blocks)

`route → route → define → define → define → define → define → define → define → define → define → define → route → derive → derive → derive → derive`

The forecasting model is the paper's most inventive move and the least justified: instead of simulating the future, it treats the world as a spatial system in which a country's banking efficiency, economic freedom and trade openness depend on its own past and on its neighbours' current values. Seven assumptions set this up - persistence, neighbour influence with global spillover, unobservables as spatial factors, capital-city distance as the metric, full trust in the Wall Street Journal/Heritage economic-freedom indices, trade as the only bilateral relation, and normal i.i.d. errors - and 163 countries with complete data are kept. The spatial weight matrix W is distance-based and row-normalised, and three equations are estimated: B = rho1 W B + B_{t-1} beta1 + e1 for banking, and the analogous models for overall freedom and trade freedom. All three are lagged-dependent-variable spatial models with a single lagged regressor, which is a very thin specification for a claim about long-run effects; the reduced form (I - rho W)^{-1} B_{t-1} beta is then what gets used for prediction. Note the identification problem the paper does not discuss: with rho and beta both estimated from two years of index data, the split between direct persistence and neighbour spillover is weakly identified, and that split is exactly what the Tunisia experiment later relies on.

## results — 6.4-6.5 Model 3 estimation, Tunisia counterfactual, sensitivity (pp. 17-18, 10 blocks)

`define → route → define → compute → interpret → read-figure → route → route → read-figure → interpret`

The estimation uses 2017 and 2018 economic-freedom data and reports beta near 0.911-1.010 and rho between -0.008 and 0.089 - so persistence is strong and neighbour spillover is small, which is the opposite of what the model was built to show. The paper then works around this: it treats Tunisia (which it says adopted digital currency officially in 2015) as a 10% shock to the indices, predicts y(2), subtracts y(1), drops Tunisia's own row because the direct effect dominates, and sorts the remaining differences into Table 8. Most countries' differences are positive, and the paper reads that as an indirect spillover. The 5% sensitivity run reproduces the sign pattern. Two things make this the weakest inference in the paper. First, the shock size (10%) is chosen, not estimated, and the counterfactual assumes digital-currency adoption is the only change in Tunisia between the two years. Second, with rho of the order of 0.02, a spillover of the magnitude claimed cannot be generated by the estimated model - the differences being read are dominated by the beta persistence term, which is common to all countries - so the 'spillover' interpretation is not supported by the coefficients in Table 8. The conclusion (banking improves, GDP rises, trade openness rises) is therefore an assumption about Tunisia exported to 163 countries.

## strengths-limitations — 7.1-7.2 Strengths and weaknesses (pp. 18-18, 9 blocks)

`ext:claim → ext:claim → ext:claim → ext:claim → ext:claim → limit → limit → limit → limit`

Five strengths, four weaknesses, each bullet fragmented into two to four measured blocks. The strengths are method claims: flexible holding ratio in Model 1, four-sector coverage in Model 1, Model 2's simplicity and lower complexity than SVM, Model 3's spatial spillover idea, and Model 3's 163-country dataset. Only the last is a claim about evidence and it is the one the estimation undermines. The weaknesses are more specific than usual and three of them land: parameter values in Model 1 are partly subjective; Model 1 ignores foreign deposits and trade, which the paper admits may bias results; Model 2's computation grows with sample size; and Model 3 assumes the freedom indices are accurate. What is missing from the list is the two limitations a reader will notice first - the first-order conditions of Model 1 are never shown, so the steady state is unverifiable, and the Tunisia counterfactual's 10% shock is chosen rather than estimated.

## conclusion — Conclusion (pp. 18-20, 1 blocks)

`ext:claim+close:handoff`

One 184-word block that restates all three models and their verdicts: the DSGE steady state is close to the real economy so central-bank issuance is feasible; the PCA isolates the access, growth, stability and security factors; the KNN classifier puts digital-currency money laundering at Low risk; and the spatial model shows positive spillovers. It ends by pivoting to the policy letter. The conclusion is consistent with the body except on one point: it says the existing economic and financial characteristics 'basically met the economic steady state', which quietly drops the comparison's weakness (two aggregate benchmarks, no tolerance). No limitations appear in the conclusion itself; they are confined to Section 7.

## memo — Policy recommendation letter to national leaders (end of paper) (pp. 20-21, 6 blocks)

`route → select → select → select → select → select+close:handoff`

Unusually, the memo is placed after the conclusion rather than at the front, and it is a genuine four-point recommendation rather than a summary. Point 1 asks every country to build a central-bank digital-currency system, on the strength of the DSGE steady state, and adds the paper's sharpest empirical claim: at a holding ratio of 1 the economy is most stable, so issuance should proceed quickly. Point 2 lists the PCA factors leaders should improve (inflation, corruption, credit rating; broad money, GDP, budget; population, unemployment; reserves ratio, exchange rate) - a checklist that is only as good as the loadings behind it. Point 3 recommends using the KNN classifier to grade money-laundering risk by sector. Point 4 is the spatial-model recommendation: early adopters should encourage neighbours, because the estimated spillovers are positive. The letter's rhetoric is stronger than its evidence at exactly the two points where the models are weakest (the 10% Tunisia shock behind point 4, and the chosen parameter values behind point 1), and it never mentions that the estimated neighbour coefficient is close to zero. Sign-off at block 278, references follow at 279.
