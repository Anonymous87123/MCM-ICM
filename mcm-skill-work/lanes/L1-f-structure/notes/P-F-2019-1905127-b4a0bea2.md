## model-establishment — 3 Analysis and model building, 3.1.1 Digital currency: price and risk (pp. 6-7, 10 blocks)

Two paragraphs of motivation and then the price-risk block. ## model-establishment — 3.1.2 Search-and-matching model under a dual monetary system (pp. 7-10, 17 blocks)

`route → define → define → define → define → define → define → read-figure → route → derive → derive → define → derive → derive → interpret → interpret → interpret`

The paper's core model and the only one that produces its own result. The setting is a one-period closed economy with representative buyers and sellers, f of buyers holding fiat, d holding digital, 1-d-f holding nothing; each buyer wants one unit of one good, each seller produces one unit at cost c; matching is random with probability min{1, i/j} so that a smaller good variety means a higher match rate. The acceptance thresholds come out as step functions of V_f - V_o against c, giving λ = 0 below the threshold, λ in (0,1) at it, and λ = 1 above - correct search-theoretic logic, and blocks 63-64 solve for the two critical acceptance levels λ̂ and μ̂ in terms of p, δ, u, c, r, pc. The three results (65-67) are the paper's most defensible output: two currencies can coexist at equal acceptance; a currency below its threshold falls into a vicious circle and is marginalised; one above it oscillates toward full acceptance. Block 67 adds an honest caveat that digital currency's low storage cost means the equilibrium coefficient may be very low. What the chapter does not do is calibrate any of p, δ, u, c, r, pc - block 122 later sets u = 1 'to make it easier to calculate', and the Table 2 parameter estimates are interpretive rather than measured, so the thresholds are symbolic until Section 4.1.2 assigns them country data. Note also that the notation on this page is badly mangled in the PDF itself (p_-o, p_-f, p_-d written as isolated subtraction fragments), which the extraction faithfully reproduces.

## model-establishment — 3.2 Government tax-revenue maximisation at the national level (pp. 10-11, 8 blocks)

`route → define → interpret → define → define → define → derive → interpret`

Short and structurally complete: objective (long-run revenue maximisation), cost-benefit discussion, intertemporal revenue function, tax rates on the two payment modes, regulatory cost C(u), and an intertemporal constraint. The economics is the interesting part and it is argued rather than derived: digital currency widens public recognition, which expands the tax base but shifts it from fiat to digital transactions; regulatory cost rises with digital volume (tracing is expensive, per the fourth assumption); and the government loses monetary-policy control, which is a systemic cost inside C(u). Blocks 72-74 then say the government sets different tax rates on the two modes because C(u) differs and because it needs macro-control - a two-instrument, two-target argument that is asserted with no derivation, and the first-order conditions are never written. The intertemporal constraint t + t(1+r) = t0 appears as a fragment with no explanation of what the subscripted t's are. Block 75 states the result verbally: the government 'induces' the optimal payment mix by choosing per-period tax rates until discounted net revenue is maximised. So the chapter supplies the paper's answer to Task 4 (countries modify banking/monetary models by tax discrimination) without ever showing the optimisation.

## model-establishment — 3.3 International trade model (3.3.1 profit difference, 3.3.2 supranational system) (pp. 11-15, 22 blocks)

`route → route → route → define → derive → derive → derive → interpret → route → ext:context → ext:context → define → route → define → define → interpret → define → define → derive → derive → interpret → interpret`

Two halves with very different quality. 3.3.1 is a clean port of Bacchetta and van Wincoop's currency-denomination result: exporter cost C(q), demand D(p), profits under producer-currency and local-currency pricing (eqs 4-5), then a second-order expansion giving the expected-profit difference as half the difference in exchange-rate variances times the second derivative of the profit function (eq 6), and the digital-currency case added the same way. The conclusion - if digital currency's exchange-rate variance against the importer is smaller than the exporter-importer variance, invoicing in digital currency maximises expected profit - is mathematically correct conditional on the expansion and is the only place the paper actually derives the international result it claims. 3.3.2 is the opposite: an OCA discussion (Mundell 1961, McKinnon 1963) that correctly notes countries will not abandon monetary independence, then a 'two-track' design where sovereign currencies continue domestically and a supranational institution issues digital currency for cross-border use, and then the key assumption is simply imposed - the digital currency's exchange rate between countries 'will always be 1' and its velocity is constant and equal across countries. With that assumption, stability is definitional, so the later claim that the supranational institution keeps value stable by supplying M_d proportional to demand D_d proves nothing. Blocks 103-111 add an inflation-target band of 1.5-2.5 per cent (citing a Chinese source) and a money-supply adjustment rule driven by net exports and net capital flows - operationally the most concrete part of the chapter, and the only place where a policy instrument is specified numerically.

## results — 4.1 Micro empirical model: threshold method and parameter estimation (pp. 15-16, 9 blocks)

`route → define → derive → define → interpret → interpret → route → define → define`

The classification exercise - the paper's answer to 'which countries will adopt digital currency' - is built from two anchors and a threshold. Zimbabwe is assumed to be at a foreign-currency equilibrium (it had abandoned its own currency), so its acceptance probability is set to 100% and the equilibrium condition is solved for the critical λ̂; the US is assumed at the dollar equilibrium with 100% acceptance, giving the critical μ̂. Every other country's measured λ and μ are then compared with the two thresholds: below λ̂ the legal-tender equilibrium cannot last; at or above it the country stays in fiat; at or above μ̂ the country converges to digital currency. That is a legitimate threshold-crossing design and it is clearly described. The two anchors are also the design's weakness: both are assumed rather than estimated, and the whole classification scales off them, so a different anchor country would move every threshold. Blocks 123-127 are Table 2, seven rows of parameter and estimation method, split one row per block; the table admits that several parameters are proxied by inflation rates and by 'national average of profit margin'. Block 122 also fixes u = 1 'without loss of accuracy', which the paper never justifies.

## results — 4.1.3 Econometric model and regression on 53 countries (pp. 16-18, 13 blocks)

`route → route → define → define → define → define → define → define → derive → source → define → check → read-figure`

This is the paper's only estimated model and it is better than the surrounding text suggests. Demand is decomposed into transaction and speculative parts plus a risk term RI; transaction demand follows from Fisher's equation with velocity assumed constant worldwide, so cross-country variation comes only from P and Y; speculative demand is a function of bitcoin trading volume TR and Google-search interest IN; risk is decomposed into legal (an ordinal variable with five levels from 'not allowed' to 'laissez-faire'), technical (trading volume and network safety) and price risk. The regression is a seven-regressor OLS on acceptance μ. Results: 53 countries, R-squared 0.282, P and IN significant at 0.1 and 0.01, the three risk variables significant. Two things deserve flagging. First, the dependent variable μ is itself a model output computed from the previous section's thresholds, so the regression explains one constructed measure with another, not adoption with adoption. Second, the paper calls 0.282 a fit that explains demand 'to a large extent', which is a misreading of an R-squared of 0.28; the honest reading is that 72% of the variation is unexplained, and the paper's own weakness section does not admit this. Data sources are concrete and checkable (Coinhills 24-hour bitcoin volume, Google Trends search heat, CPI, GDP), which is more than most F papers manage.

## results — 4.2 Monte Carlo macro simulation, 4.3 discussion of outside factors (pp. 19-21, 8 blocks)

`route → define → derive → read-figure → interpret → read-figure → route → interpret`

The simulation is described in one 335-word block and reported in two figures. Setup: two markets (a fiat market with exchange-rate conversion and policy-affected rates, and a digital market), exchange-rate fluctuations drawn from normal distributions, and repeated runs from different initial trading proportions. Result: digital currency's market share stabilises at 65.21% regardless of the initial share, and if the supranational central bank loses regulatory capacity the share collapses below its starting level. The 65.21% is reported to four significant figures with no confidence interval, no number of runs, no parameter table and no sensitivity, so it cannot be reproduced or assessed - and since the digital currency's exchange rate is assumed fixed at 1 by the model, the stable share is largely a property of that assumption. Section 4.3 (one 706-word block) is the paper's most concrete policy discussion: commercial banks lose the settlement agency function but keep deposit and lending; internet-finance scale effects push toward consolidation; monetary policy transmission weakens; and there are implications for less-developed economies. It is reasoned prose with no model behind it, and the paper's own strengths section calls these 'exogenous factors in section 4.3' - an accurate label.

## strengths-limitations — 5.1 Strengths and weaknesses (pp. 22-23, 6 blocks)

`route → ext:claim → ext:claim → ext:claim → limit → limit`

Three strengths and two weaknesses, each bullet fragmented into four or five measured blocks because the PDF sets every wrapped line as a separate text run - so five coded groups cover what are visually five bullets. The strengths are: acceptability is the right core variable, and the model is flexible enough to absorb added factors; the DMP-based mechanism explains government attitudes and some policies; and the theoretical preparation (Fisher plus Keynes) is 'sufficient'. The last is the least defensible, since the Fisher equation is used only in differenced form and Keynes's theory only to motivate two demand components. The weaknesses are the honest ones: the framework is large and the sub-models are loosely linked, which is exactly true (the three models share notation but no equilibrium condition); and the global decentralised market does not exist, so the model has a weak realistic foundation and the macro theory is abstract. That second admission is the paper's most accurate self-assessment and it applies specifically to the 65.21% simulation.
