## assumptions — 2 Assumptions and Justifications (pp. 4-5, 9 blocks)

`limit → limit → limit → limit → limit → limit → limit → limit → route`

21 blocks, 218 words, four assumptions with explanations, every paragraph cut one printed line per block (average 10 words). Assumption 1 (32) is that GDP and GGDP grow at the same rate, justified by assuming governments set the GGDP growth target “essentially identical” to the old GDP target (33-36) — this is the assumption that later lets the paper re-run history as if the switch had happened in 2000. ## results — 6.3 Assessment Model Results (pp. 13-14, 6 blocks)

`derive → check → route → interpret → interpret → read-figure`

6 blocks, 275 words. 142 gives the fitted equation: “CI = −1.469 × 10^-14 GGDP + 1.835” (15), and 143 the fit, R² = 0.987. The slope is worth recording exactly as printed: CI moves by 1.5e-14 per dollar, so across the whole range of world GGDP the fitted CI changes by less than one unit — the high R² comes from the enormous variance of GGDP, not from a strong relationship, and the paper reads the coefficient as evidence that the two quantities are tightly linked. 144 announces Figure 7, and the two findings follow: globally ΔCI is negative under both indicators, but the GGDP curve sits above the GDP curve, so deterioration slows by “31.…” percent on average (145) — the same 31.57% the memo repeats; and during the subprime crisis and COVID-19 the advantage shrinks to “only 5.23%” (146). Finding (2) is the more credible of the two and the paper treats it as a caveat rather than as evidence about the mechanism: the GGDP advantage disappears exactly when output collapses, which is what one would expect if the effect runs through the denominator rather than through policy.

## model-establishment — 7 Cost-Benefit Analysis Model (7.1 Potential Upside, 7.2 Potential Downside, 7.3 Comparison) (pp. 14-17, 17 blocks)

`route → route → mechanism → derive → interpret → ext:context → source → read-figure → read-figure → read-figure → read-figure → derive → define → interpret → interpret → read-figure → interpret`

17 blocks, 1141 words — the longest chapter and the one that carries the is-it-worthwhile verdict. 149 sets the question, 150 gives the upside as a mechanism (to raise GGDP countries must cut NRDC and EDC, which slows vegetation loss and sea-level rise), and 151 introduces the downside as a correction term, PD = ΔGDP − ΔGDP′ + Pt (17), where Pt is multilateral resistance. 152 defines GDP loss with Qatar as the example — a country whose growth “is contingent upon the extensive carbon emissions” and whose status would suffer under GGDP. 153 surveys the resistance literature (fixed effects, remoteness, gravity models) before 154 states the paper's own method: cluster countries on GGDP per capita against the rate of climate deterioration, read attitudes off the clusters, and estimate resistance from cluster membership. 155-161 then read the K-means result off a scatter the text calls Figure 7, which is the wrong number — Figure 7 is the ΔCI impact chart (caption at 147) and the scatter never gets a printed caption at all: the green lower-right cluster supports the switch; the orange cluster accepts but will not lead; the red cluster has high degradation and would need to move up the y-axis, with the distance formalised as Pt = k Σ Distance(xi) + b (18); and the dense blue lower-left cluster simply follows the trend. 162 gives the verdict: “the PU is smaller than the PD for almost every year since 2000, indicating that the switch to GGDP is worthwhile on a global scale”. Two things must be recorded. First, the resistance term is a distance in a two-axis scatter multiplied by an unstated k plus an unstated b, so PD is not a monetary quantity at all, yet it is compared year by year against PU, which is a climate-mitigation gain in index points; the comparison in 162 is therefore between two numbers in different units, and Figure 9's crossing has no interpretable scale. Second, 164 reads the 2008 dip in resistance as evidence that “GGDP's ability to slow down the rate of change of GDP ... can help mitigate economic losses during times of crisis”, which is the opposite reading of the same crisis episode that 146 used as a caveat.
