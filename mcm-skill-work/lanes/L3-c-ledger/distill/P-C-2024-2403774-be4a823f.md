## 5. Reusable writing action

**Define a latent quantity by the failure mode of its absence.** The chapter has to introduce something no data column holds, and instead of a verbal definition it writes a definition plus its contrapositive in a single sentence - so the reader is given not just what momentum *is* but what the world would look like if it were not there, which is exactly what the later tests try to rule out.

> We make the following assumption: if the game state is related to scoring and losing, it can be reflected through momentum, completing the mapping from the distribution of game states to the distribution of scoring situations, establishing a relationship between the two distributions; conversely, if there is no relationship between them, such a mapping would be impossible to achieve.

The move is structural, not rhetorical: the first half names a mapping between two distributions (`game states` to `scoring situations`), the `conversely` half states the null that a random-match baseline is built to instantiate, and the whole thing is flagged as `the following assumption` so that the reader knows a premise has been taken rather than a fact claimed. It also does the work that a later section needs - the random-scenario generator of unit 4 is the mechanical form of "no relationship between them".

Copy the shape whenever a task names something unobservable: one sentence, `<quantity> is implicitly embedded in <observable state>`, `we make the following assumption: if <observable> is related to <target>, it can be reflected through <quantity>, completing the mapping ... ; conversely, if there is no relationship, such a mapping would be impossible`. Then, when the paper needs a control condition, it already has one: build the converse case rather than argue about it.

What the chapter does not do is use the assumption it just wrote. The converse is never tested directly - the comparison in unit 4 is between real matches and simulated random ones on metric values, and no sentence returns to the distribution-mapping language that justified the model in the first place.
