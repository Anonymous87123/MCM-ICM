## 5. Reusable writing action

Gloss every symbol of a display in one sentence, in reading order, immediately after the display. The chapter does it
once and it is the cleanest sentence in the paper: "Where Wij is the weight from the input layer to the hidden layer,
aj is the hidden layer threshold, L is the number of hidden layer nodes, Wjk is the weight from the hidden layer to
the output layer, bk is the output layer threshold." - and it follows (1) and (2) with nothing in between, so a reader
who has just met two stacked summation formulas can name all four indices without looking back.

What the move buys is that the indices of an equation become words. (1) and (2) are dense with `i`, `j`, `k`, `n`,
`L`, `m`; the gloss picks four of them, gives each a noun and an article ("the weight", "the threshold") and tells the
reader which layer each belongs to. Because the sentences are parallel - `W` is "the weight from X to Y", `a`/`b` is
"the threshold" - the gloss can be read as a map of the network's edges, which is exactly what Figure 4 then draws.

The failure is that the gloss's coverage is chosen by convenience rather than by need. It defines `L`, a mere
summation limit, and it leaves `n`, `m` and `f` undefined - `f` is the activation that (1) applies and that (4)
differentiates as `(1 − H_j)`. It never mentions `x_i`, although (4) multiplies by it and (1) appears to be missing
it. So the one move that would have caught the equations' defects - naming every symbol and checking that the same
name means the same thing on both sides - is performed on four of the nine symbols, and the two symbols that are
wrong are the two it skips. A gloss is a test, not a courtesy: run it over the whole display, in order, and let the
symbols that cannot be given a distinct noun be the defect report.
