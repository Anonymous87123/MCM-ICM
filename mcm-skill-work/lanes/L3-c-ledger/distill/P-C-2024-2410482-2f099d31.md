## 5. Reusable writing action

**Calibrate a new metric with two extremes before you define it, so the definition arrives as a summary rather than a stipulation.** The chapter has just built a machine that computes `P(win the match | score)` at every moment. Instead of defining its momentum index directly, it first runs two cases through that machine:

- the tiebreak: `A tennis match of two players A and B is going to the key moment to decide the outcome ... So, the outcome of this game will make the change of the winning probability up to 100%.` (block 80)
- the opening game: `if A wins, the winning rate of the game increases from 0.5 to 0.6, if A loses, the winning rate of the game is only reduced to 0.4 ... The influence of this game on the winning rate is only 0.6-0.4=0.2.` (block 81)

Only then does the definition appear, as the name for what the two examples have in common: `In other words, Leberage can be defined as "winning rate difference". Here is Leberage 𝐿𝑎for 𝑔𝑎𝑚𝑒𝑎: 𝐿𝑎= 𝑃(𝑤𝑖𝑛𝑡ℎ𝑒𝑚𝑎𝑡𝑐ℎ|𝑤𝑖𝑛𝑔𝑎𝑚𝑒𝑎) −𝑃(𝑤𝑖𝑛𝑡ℎ𝑒𝑚𝑎𝑡𝑐ℎ|𝑙𝑜𝑠𝑠𝑔𝑎𝑚𝑒𝑎) (4)` (block 81), and the reason it is legal to compute at all is stated one block later: `The introduction of Markov Model in the previous chapter provides a shortcut for calculating conditional probability.` (block 83).

The action is three moves in order - name the range the index can take by showing its maximum, show the ordinary case, then write the formula as the thing both cases already instantiated. It works because the reader has seen the number 100% and the number 0.2 before being asked to accept a symbol, so the formula has a scale attached to it on arrival.

**What it costs.** The coinage is printed three ways (`Leberage` in the definition and the bullet, `Lerberage` in block 86, `leberage` in block 84), it carries no units, and the second case's arithmetic (`0.6-0.4=0.2`) is what the reader remembers rather than the definition. And the index is uncomputable without the previous chapter's model - the calibration is honest about that (`a shortcut`), but the paper never says what to do for a reader who wants the momentum of a match whose win probabilities were never computed.
