## 5. Reusable writing action

**Make one signed curve carry three different questions: sign for who, distance for how much, zero crossing for when.**

Most modelling chapters define a quantity and then have to introduce a second one for every new question. This chapter defines the instantaneous scoring rate once, as the derivative of the interpolated score difference (equation (1)), and then reads the same number three ways without ever redefining it:

> when the instantaneous scoring rate is lower than 0, it means that the player's scoring will be reduced at this point, whereas the position away from 0 responds to the extent to which the player scores or loses points at this point - -The further the distance from 0, the higher or lower the player's scoring rate then, thus allowing identification of which player is performing better at a given point in time.

> Based on the above image, we colored the scatterplot according to which player served first at that score, and the coloring results are shown in Figure 4

> It can be seen that the moving average can effectively reflect the trend of the Instantaneous Scoring Rate, so we choose the moving average as the momentum of the match. Therefore, when the average line crosses the 0 axis, it means that the momentum of the game has changed at this time.

Sign answers *who is ahead on the flow*; distance from zero answers *by how much*; a zero crossing answers *when the match turned*. The colouring by who served is a fourth reading of the same points, and it costs one sentence because the quantity was already signed - the reader does not have to relearn the axis.

Why it works: a signed quantity has more information per unit of notation than an unsigned one, and a paper that names its zero explicitly can hand the same axis to a test, a plot and a threshold rule. The chapter's later smoothing step (equation (4)) inherits all of it unchanged: the moving average is over the same signed rate, so the zero-crossing rule needs no new argument.

How to reuse: when you define a derived quantity, choose the sign convention so that the zero has a meaning in the domain (`momentum of the game has changed at this time`), then write one sentence per question that names that reading, and never introduce a second symbol for the same curve at a coarser smoothing level - say `moving average of the same rate` instead.

What it costs, and the chapter does not say so: the three readings are only coherent if the zero is stable, and this chapter's zero is not - it is the mean of a curve whose amplitude the author calls `extremely large`, and the sign of an ISR at any moment depends on which ten points the window happened to include. That is exactly why the paper needs the ten-point average in the first place, and the advice built on top (`when the average line crosses the 0 axis`) therefore reports a crossing of a smoothed statistic as a change in the match. Copy the tripling of one signed curve; do not copy the silence about what zero means after smoothing.
