Arithmetic and definition that do not close:

- `(1)` and `(7)`'s `MA_t` sum `i = 0` to `n`, which is `n + 1` terms, and divide by `n`.
- `(7)`'s dispersion is not a dispersion: `MD_t = √( Σ_{i=0}^{n} (w_i MA_t) ∗ (w_i − MA_t) / n )`. The squared deviation is printed as a product of the price and the mean, so the term is `w_i · MA_t · (w_i − MA_t)` - one factor of the bracket lost its minus sign and the square became a multiplication. `MB_t`, `UP_t`, `DN_t` are then computed from `MB_t = MA_{t−1}`, yesterday's mean, which no sentence explains, and the gloss names only `n` and `k`, leaving `MA`, `MD`, `MB`, `UP`, `DN` undefined.
- `(2)` makes `(1)`'s signal vacuous. With `AMA = DMA/10`, "When DMA is greater than AMA, it is a buy signal" is true exactly when `DMA > 0`; the comparison adds no band, no cushion and no scale, and the `/10` is never justified. What remains is the sign of a difference of two sums, and since `(1)` sums `n₁` and `n₂` raw prices rather than averaging them, that difference is dominated by which window is longer.
- `(6)`'s signal is inverted with respect to `(6)`. As printed, `WMS = (w − wl_n)/(wh_n − wl_n) ∗ 100` is `100` when the close sits at the cycle high, yet the gloss reads "When WMS is above I, it is oversold and is a buy signal. When WMS is below O, it is overbought and a sell signal" - a high reading called oversold. The RSI sentence three pages earlier has the polarity right ("When RSI is greater than O ... a sell signal"), so the two neighbouring definitions of the same template disagree about which end of the scale means what.
- `(8)`'s cash line runs backwards: `C_{k+1} = C_k + (1−α)x_{k+1}B_{k+1}p_{k+1} − C_k q_{k+1}` adds cash when the buy indicator fires and subtracts all cash when the sell indicator fires. Buying consumes cash and selling returns it; both branches are printed with the opposite sign.
- `(10)`, the line-by-line restatement of the same recursion, swaps the two indicators: `C_{k+1} = C_k + (1−α)x_{k+1}B_{k+1}q_{k+1} − C_k p_{k+1}`. Same symbols, same page-spread, opposite assignment of `p` and `q`. The prose gloss above it - "the total cash value on day `k+1` is equal to that day's cash plus the cash received from selling bitcoins on that day minus the cash spent on buying bitcoins minus the transaction costs" - matches `(10)`'s directions and not `(8)`'s.
- The cash update reads `B_{k+1}` and `p_{k+1}` into the equation for `C_{k+1}` in both versions, so the next day's holdings determine the next day's cash, and `(11)` then defines `B_{k+1}` from `C_k`. The two recursions are mutually forward-referencing.
- The optimization has no decision variables. `Z = max M_n` is maximised subject to a system whose only free symbols are `n`, `I` and `O`, and nothing in `(8)` declares what is being chosen; `(14)`'s `1 ≤ n ≤ 100, 1 ≤ I < O ≤ 100` bounds three parameters the model never mentions in its objective. `n` is worse than unbound: it is the RSI window in `(8)`'s indicators and simultaneously the terminal day of the objective `M_n`, so the trading horizon is set equal to the indicator's look-back.
- `(15)` is announced with a promise the display immediately breaks: "the rest of the parameter settings and symbolic meanings are the same as those described above, so we will not repeat them here" - and then prints the entire nine-line system again under `gd` subscripts. It is the same model twice, and one of the two copies had to be printed anyway, so the sentence buys nothing and costs the reader the check of whether the two copies agree.
- `(19)` sums `t` terms and divides by `t − 1`, in all three lines. The covariance `σ_t^{bg} = (1/(t−1)) Σ_{i=1}^{t} (ρ_i^{btc} − ρ̄_t^{btc})(ρ_i^{gd} − ρ̄_t^{gd})` also carries a bar on `ρ̄_t` that appears in no display before it and is glossed nowhere, and the whole display is given one numeral for three separate formulas.
- `(20)` cannot do what the section says it does. The objective is `Min σ_t = √[(σ_t)²]`, the square root of the square of its own left-hand side - algebraically `Min σ_t`. There is no expected return anywhere: no `μ`, no return vector, no target-return constraint, no `Σ H = 1`. With `0 ≤ H^btc + H^gd ≤ 1` and `H ≥ 0` the minimiser is `H^btc = H^gd = 0`, which is the position of holding nothing, and the prose introducing the voting system says the point is "In order to maximize the return of bitcoin and gold investment".

## 5. Reusable writing action

Close every indicator definition with one sentence that names the threshold and the side, so that the display becomes
a decision the moment it is read. The chapter does it six times in the same words, and the sentence is what makes the
voting layer possible at all - by p14 the factor names alone are enough to write "we choose DMA, RSI, MACD, TRIX, WR
... to form a voting system". Two of the six, verbatim:

"When RSI is greater than O, it is considered as a sell signal; when RSI is less than I, it is considered as a buy
signal. n, O and I are all parameters to be determined."

"When DMA is greater than AMA, it is a buy signal; conversely, when DMA is less than AMA, it is a sell signal."

The move is cheap and it is the right one: a factor is only usable inside a vote if the reader can point at the number
that flips it, and the sentence supplies exactly that - the two threshold symbols, the direction, and the promise that
they will be chosen later rather than asserted now.

The failure is that the sentence is written from the English name of the factor instead of from its own algebra, and
nothing in the paragraph forces the two to agree. DMA's sentence is void because `(2)` sets `AMA = DMA/10`: "DMA is
greater than AMA" is true exactly when `DMA > 0`, so the comparison names a threshold that cannot be anything but zero,
while `(1)` sums two different numbers of raw prices so that `DMA` is mostly a statement about which window is longer.
WR's sentence is inverted against `(6)`: as printed, `WMS` is `100` when the close sits at the cycle high, and the
sentence calls that "oversold ... a buy signal" - the same template that got RSI's polarity right, applied one page
later to a formula whose orientation is the other way. Both defects would have been caught by reading the sentence
backwards over the display: take the extreme value of the right-hand side, ask which word the sentence attaches to it,
and compare. A signal sentence is a claim about the sign of a derivative; write it last, derive it from the formula,
and let the formula, not the literature's description of it, choose which end of the scale means buy.
