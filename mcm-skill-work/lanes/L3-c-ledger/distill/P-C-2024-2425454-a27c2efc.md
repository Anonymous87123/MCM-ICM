**Define the unobservable with a three-line fictional vignette, then hand the scoreboard's identity to the sign of the quantity you just defined.** The chapter does not open with a formula for leverage. It opens with a point:

- `• Current Scenario: Player A is down 0-15 in the current game, his real-time winning probablity (abbreviated as RTWP in the latter.) is 69%.` (129-130)
- `• Define Realistic Scenario: If player A wins this point, the score becomes 30-30 and his RTWP becomes 71%.` (131)
- `• Imagine the Opposite Scenario: if player A loses this point, the score becomes 15-40 and his RTWP becomes 65%.` (132)
- `• Calculating Leverage: By comparing the change in A's RTWP in these two scenarios, the Leverage for this point is calculated to be 6%.` (133-134)

and then labels the whole illustration `Note: The above data is fictional.` (135). The definition arrives after the reader has already computed it.

The second half of the action is the encoding: `We set Li to represent the leverage of the ith point. The positive or negative sign does not indicate the magnitude but only signifies which player gained the point. Throughout the subsequent analysis, we consistently calculate the leverage with respect to Player A. If Li > 0, it means that player A won the ith point; if Li < 0, it means that player B won the ith point; if Li = 0, it means that the ith point did not result in a change in winning percentage.` (136-137). One signed number per point then carries two pieces of information - who won and by how much - so a single exponentially weighted average (139) is enough to define momentum, and one more hand-off sentence turns that average into the paper's object: `Finally, by focusing on the fluctuations in momentum, we can capture "the flow of play".` (141).

The action is worth copying because it is cheap and it survives scrutiny: a reader can reproduce the metric by hand from three percentages, and can read the running average without a key. It is also the reason this paper can define momentum in three blocks (S5 is 133 words) where the two papers before it spent a unit and a half.

**What it costs.** The zero case is undecidable: `Li = 0` means both "no change in winning percentage" and "we cannot tell who won", so the encoding has a value that carries neither of its two meanings, and nothing in the chapter says what happens to a 0-leverage point when the average is taken. The sign convention is also undone two blocks later by the piecewise split `GA i = Li, Li ⩾ 0 / 0, Li < 0` (138), which separates identity from magnitude again - so the reader who built momentum out of a signed average now has to carry two vectors, and the smoothing coefficient `α ∈ (0, 1)` is then justified by prose alone (`the importance of a certain points decreases exponentially as the game progresses`) with `α` set to `0.33` by a sentence that names no criterion (`by testing the leverage defined by the handicap winning percentage, it better reflects the fluctuation of the game`, 142).
