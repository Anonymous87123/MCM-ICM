## 5. Reusable writing action

**Turn an existential question into a distance between two sequences.**

Task 2 has to prove that momentum exists, which is not a thing a competition paper can measure directly. The
chapter never tries. It states the negation as a runnable model, runs it, and then compares the two outputs:

> `For task 2, our approach involves employing a method of contradiction by assum- ing that momentum does not exist in tennis matches. If the conclusions drawn from this assumption significantly diverge from actual data, it would indicate the fallacy of the hypothesis.`

> `To test the idea that momentum doesn't affect match outcomes, we simulate games without considering momentum. Our simulation uses Gaussian noise, player strength, and serving advantage which come from the model I, as the only inputs.`

and then the sentence that does the actual work, which is a change of problem, not a result:

> `By doing so, we transform the problem of comparing two match's results into the problem of calculating the similarity of two sequences.`

Why it works: the first sentence fixes what evidence would count as failure, the second makes the null world cheap
to generate, and the third swaps an unanswerable question for a computable one. The weakness sits in the sentence
that describes the two sequences - `one that might includes momentum effects and another that assumes outcomes are
entirely random` - because the momentum-bearing side is never defined as an algorithm, only as "might include", so
the distance that follows is between a specified null and an unspecified alternative. The paper also never states
what distance would count as `significantly diverge`, which was promised in the first sentence. A reader copying the
move should carry the sentence the paper omits: pre-register the threshold before running either simulation.
