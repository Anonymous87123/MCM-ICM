## 5. Reusable writing action

**Give every model a strength clause and a failure clause, and make the failure clause name a specific thing in this dataset - then fuse the two.** Block 111 is the template, and it is worth copying almost verbatim because the shape is so legible: `Logistic regression (LR) is a widely used model that excels in simplicity and interpretability, effectively estimating probabilities when the relationships between predictors and outcomes are linear. However, it struggles with non-linear patterns and complex feature interactions that are often present in Olympic data, such as historical variability or regional effects.` The strength is generic and short; the failure is *this* data's failure, with `historical variability or regional effects` supplied as instances. Block 112 then does the same to the alternative - `RF captures non-linear relationships and complex interactions while ranking feature importance, making it suitable for diverse datasets. However, RF can overfit and lacks the transparency of LR` - so the second model's failure is a defect *the first model had*, not an independent flaw. That symmetry is what makes 113's `To leverage the strengths of both models, we adopted a hybrid approach that combines the interpretability of LR with the flexibility of RF` read as a conclusion rather than as decoration: each half of the hybrid has already been shown to cover the other's hole.

The chapter uses the identical four-move shape a second time, in G1, for the regression side: `The Multiple Linear Regression (MLR) model is simple and interpretable, effective for linear relationships but limited in capturing non-linear dynamics like the host effect` (73), then FNN's `excels in capturing non-linear patterns`, then `Combining MLR's simplicity with FNN's adaptability, we propose a hybrid approach for enhanced medal prediction`. Four sentences, no citation, no benchmark - and the reader knows exactly why two models were fused instead of one.

**The third move is what most MCM papers omit.** A paper that says "we use LR because it is interpretable" states a strength with no failure clause, so the choice is unjustified; a paper that says "LR struggles with non-linear patterns" without an instance is a textbook disclaimer that the writer cannot be held to. Naming `historical variability or regional effects` costs five words and converts a truism into a claim about Olympic data that the following sections then have to honour.

**What it costs.** The fusion is never given a selection rule. Nothing in the chapter reports what happens without it: there is no LR-only baseline, no FNN-only baseline and no ablation, so `enhanced medal prediction` is asserted, not measured, and the fusion weight arrives as `wMLR = PE2... ≈0.6` with no derivation and no alternative values tried. The pattern is also applied where it does not fit: block 141 promises `a comprehensive evaluation framework based on four key indicators` and then the weighting is chosen by `Min-Max` and `PCA` with no failure clause for either, so a reader who learned the shape from 111-113 expects an argument and gets two method names. And the second fusion repeats the first - 5.2.3's `Pˆi = wMLR · PMLR,i + wNN · PNN,i` and 6.4's classifier average are the same weighted-sum device applied to a regression and a classification problem, which is defensible, but the chapter never says so, so it reads as two unrelated hybrids rather than one idea used twice.
