## 5. Reusable writing action

**Declare the property that makes your table look wrong, in one sentence, in the paragraph before the table prints - and make the declaration specific enough that a reader can test it.**

The action is a single sentence that sits between the last block of section 3.3.3 and the heading of section 3.4, on leaf 12 (block 94), one leaf ahead of the table it defends:

> Our prediction intervals for Gold, Silver, Bronze, and Total are predicted separately, so the Total prediction interval is not determined by the intervals for the three medal categories.

Why it is reusable: the reader who reaches Table 8 on leaf 13 and adds the three lower bounds has already been told not to, so a table whose Total columns do not equal the sum of their parts arrives as a stated modelling choice rather than as a mistake - and the three-task paper keeps its whole exhibit inventory on twelve pages of prose. It also costs nothing to place: the sentence is a one-line consequence of the method (`predicted separately`), so it does not need a new subsection, a footnote or a limitation section.

Why it must be exact: the declaration is only worth making if it names the statistic and the objects, and this one does - `prediction intervals`, `Gold, Silver, Bronze, and Total`. The paper then fails its own test, because nine of the thirty offsets between a Total bound and the sum of its three component bounds are exact whole medals (`+6.0` and `+11.0` on the lower side, `-4.0`, `-5.0`, `-8.0` and `-10.0` on the upper), which is what hand-editing looks like and is not what four independent estimates look like. A reusable version of the move therefore needs both halves: say the parts are independent, and then let the printed digits be decimals that no one could have summed - the sentence buys the reader's patience once, and the table either confirms it or spends it.
