## 5. Reusable writing action

Give every mechanism its own subsection, and close each one with a single sentence that fires it - a condition the
reader can check against the display without reading anything else. The chapter does it for all nine, and it is the only
reason nine mechanisms fit in four pages without a single cross-reference. Three of the nine, verbatim:

"If S > 0, the model sells."

"The condition for an extreme market condition is the gradient being over 5 times the average gradient over the history
of the model."

"If E is positive, then the price of an asset is growing rapidly and an extreme market condition takes over."

The move is the right one and it is cheap: a rule is only usable if the reader can point at the line that flips it, and
the sentence supplies exactly that - the quantity, the inequality, and the direction. It also makes the chapter testable,
because a fired condition is a claim that can be contradicted later.

Two things break it here. First, the sentence and the display do not speak the same language: "over 5 times" is printed
in prose, the display carries `C`, and `C` is glossed only as "a scaling factor" - so the one number the sentence
promises is the one symbol the display will not give. `C` is then reused in 3.9 as "a scalar constant" in a different
formula, and no reader can tell whether the two are the same. Second, closed fire-sentences do not add up to a procedure:
nine rules, each with its own trigger, and the only ordering in the chapter is the 130-word paragraph of 3.8 - which
opens by checking the no-buy and extreme flags and never says what happens when a flag is up, so 3.5's own release
condition ("until the price has regressed to a percentage of its maximum value ... or a price ∆P below its maximum") is
tested by no line of the program. A fire-sentence has to be written twice: once in prose, with the number spelled out,
and once as an edge in the procedure that the branch order can actually reach.
