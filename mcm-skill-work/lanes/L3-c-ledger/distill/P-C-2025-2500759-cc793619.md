## 5. Reusable writing action

**Before presenting the test you actually ran, name the two obvious alternatives and reject each in a sentence.** The chapter does not simply announce a signed-rank test. It spends a paragraph on the choice and two bullets on the reasons:

- `The decision to utilize the signed rank test, rather than employing a one-sample t-test or defining a binary variable for "having won a gold medal" to apply logistic regression or binary classification machine learning algorithms, is based on the following considerations.` (52)
- `• The signed rank test is a non-parametric method that does not require any assumptions regarding the distribution of the underlying population. In contrast, the t-test is a parametric method that assumes that the data originate from a normally distributed population ... As such, the non-parametric test is more aligned with real-world scenarios, thus minimizing the likelihood of substantial errors.` (53-57)
- `• Implementing a binary variable for "winning a gold medal" to design a classification algorithm and calculate the estimated probability of countries achieving a "breakthrough of zero" would replace the quantitative variable of gold medal counts with a qualitative variable. This approach neglects the correlation between feature variables and may lead to biased and unreasonable results.` (58-62)

The action is cheap and it buys the paper something most modelling sections never get: a reader who was going to ask "why not a t-test" has been answered before asking, and the answer is about the data's shape rather than about convenience. It also frames the test as a *choice* among three instruments applied to the same bootstrap sample, which makes the later decision rule (`If the P-value is less than or equal to 0.05 ... the country is statistically unable to achieve a "breakthrough of zero"`, 76) read as the end of an argument rather than as a definition. The same move is repeated at smaller scale for the tuner: `Notably, the subsample parameter remained constant throughout the tuning process, set at 0.5 to ensure a reasonable sampling proportion.`

**What it costs.** The rejection is by assertion, not by evidence: nothing in the chapter compares the three instruments on this data, so the argument that a t-test's normality assumption fails could be checked in one line from the same bootstrap sample and is not. Worse, **the decision rule converts a p-value into a probability statement, and then rewards the larger p-value**: `with probabilities of 0.847 and 0.684` is read off the p-value column of Table 3, and the text adds that `a larger P-value indicates a smaller probability of committing a Type II error, thereby increasing the likelihood of realizing the "breakthrough of zero"`, so the country with the largest p-value is declared the most likely to win gold. A p-value under `H0 : θ = 1` is not the probability that the country wins a gold medal, and inverting the usual direction (large p = good news) leaves a reader unable to tell what would count as evidence against the claim. The rule also arrives without its own sample size: the chapter says `we independently run the constructed XGBoost model 1,000 times`, and two of Table 3's statistics (`538231` and `532048`) exceed the largest signed-rank sum 1,000 draws can produce (`1000 × 1001 / 2 = 500500`), so the test's effective `n` is neither printed nor consistent with the bootstrap it is built on.
