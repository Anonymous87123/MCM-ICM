## 5. Reusable writing action

Instantiate the mechanism on a fictional asset before naming a single parameter, and say out loud what the
idealisation switches off. Unit 2 does exactly this: "As a toy instance, the current price of an E.G. Coin,
which only exists in the demo but not in the real world, is 6 dollars and we start the grid model with 4 E.G.
Coins and 10 dollars. We ignore the transaction costs." Four transactions follow - a buy at 4.57, a sell of one
coin, a sell of two - each written as arithmetic the reader can redo, and the four prices in the toy are the
four markers printed inside Figure 1 (6.0, 4.57, 5.26, 7.48).

What makes it reusable is the order and the admissions: the mechanism is stated as a crossing rule, then
demonstrated on an asset that cannot be mistaken for data ("which only exists in the demo but not in the real
world"), then a limitation is named before it can bias the reader ("We ignore the transaction costs"), and only
after that does the chapter introduce `T_start`, `T_end`, `p_max`, `p_min`, `num_in`, `num_0` and `num`. The
limitation is not decorative: it becomes the reason the width cannot be shrunk later, where block 82 says that
"if we apply transaction cost on every trade, we have cannot make the width for every grid to be too small[12]".

The failure is instructive too. The toy's last valuation prints `5.43 + 5.26 + 2 × 7.48 = 25.95` where the
summands give 25.65, and the figure's grid lines are one dollar apart while the text's crossing rule says five.
A worked instance buys the reader's trust and then has to survive being checked - which is the point of writing
one - so the numbers in a toy belong to the same review as the equations.
