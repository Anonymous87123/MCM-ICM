## 5. Reusable writing action

**Price the baseline's failure in the unit the reader can check, then let the size of the over-run write the requirements for the next model - "8 golds in table tennis" against "there are only 5" does more work than any amount of calling the baseline inadequate.**

The chapter runs the move once, and it is the cleanest instance in this lane because the number it uses is not an error metric but a physical impossibility:

> The final loss dropped to 0.0058, indicating good convergence, however, plain LSTM yielded poor results, as indicated in the following two figures. Fig. 4 repeated the medal count in 2024, and is a clear overfit, while Fig. 5 predicts that China will earn 8 Olympic Gold medals in Table Tennis in 2028, while we know that there are only 5, and China can earn at most 5 (as it did in 2024). Therefore, we must take into consideration varying in the number of events held in each discipline, and avoid overfitting. Also, we should consider host effect. All of these will be addressed in our advanced XGBoost Model in the following section.

Why it is reusable: the loss curve cannot fail in a way a reader feels - 0.0058 is small, and `good convergence` is exactly what a broken model also says. The sentence that follows swaps the metric for a count of medals, and 8 against 5 needs no statistical training to reject. Then the two remedies are named in the order the reader has just discovered them (`varying in the number of events held in each discipline` is what makes 8 impossible; `host effect` is what a ratio-based model has not yet looked at), so the next section's two design choices arrive as consequences rather than as a list. The pattern generalises to any model whose output is a quantity with a hard ceiling: report the headline fit, then print one predicted value beside the ceiling it violates.

**The same paper shows the second half of the move at the host-effect seam, and the two together are the template.** Having established that `approximately 90% of countries exhibit an improvement`, the chapter refuses its own global average by naming which countries drive it:

> However, the countries with increases over 50% tend to be those with relatively fewer points (the Soviet Union is excluded from this analysis as it has since transitioned into Russia and other countries). Therefore, it is unreasonable to anticipate a substantial increase for the United States, a major scoring country and the host of the 2028 Olympics. Considering that the U.S. has previously observed a decrease of 7% in scores when hosting, we deemed it reasonable to predict that the U.S. will have a hosting nation increase between 0% and 20% for the year 2024. In our predictive model, we incorporated a scoring increase of 15%.

Why it is reusable: a summary statistic is turned into a decision by three explicit steps - identify the subpopulation the average is really describing, say why the object of interest is not in it, and then pick one number inside a stated band and use that number everywhere downstream. A reader can audit `15%` because the band `0% to 20%` and the `7%` decrease are both on the page.

Why it fails the second time, in a way worth copying the fix of: the move is not repeated. The Cox model arrives with no baseline and no ceiling to violate; the Great Coach Effect section adds two thresholds and an Isolation Forest with no statement of what the simpler version gets wrong, so `differential points` is defined by an indicator `(20)` whose thresholds `S_T` and `S_Tγ` are never given values, only justified as minimising score variance - and the paper's own sensitivity analysis is Section 9, six sections later. And the one number that is chosen, the `15%`, is written into the model and never varied, while the sentence that sets it says `for the year 2024` in a paragraph whose subject is the host of the 2028 Games; the page prints 2024. A reusable version of the template runs at every model seam, states the baseline's failure in the unit the reader can check, and then carries the chosen number and its band forward together, so that the reader can see later whether the sensitivity analysis varied the band or only the model.
