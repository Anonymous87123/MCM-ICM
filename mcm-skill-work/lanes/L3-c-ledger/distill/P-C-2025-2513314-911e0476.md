The column otherwise behaves: the gold-group mean of the six breakthrough probabilities is 0.4406 against a total-group mean of 0.2895, which supports the prose's claim that the gold path is the harder one, and `Figure 4`'s prose figures track the real 2024 tallies exactly, so the paper's data handling is sound where the arithmetic is visible and broken where a table is chained from another table.

**The stamp is the largest single number in the pack and it is not the paper's fault.** 687 `m` glyphs at 1.38 pt, alpha 0, on a 37×37 lattice inside a 68×68 pt block, repeated on every leaf - it renders pure white (minimum channel 255, zero pixels below 250) and it is counted by every text extractor. That one artefact explains the 2,324 word field, the 749-token block `65`, the 319-token block `73`, and the two blocks that are more stamp than prose.

## 5. Reusable writing action

**Open a model section by rejecting each rival family in one clause that names the property it breaks, then let the chosen model's two components answer exactly the two properties still standing.**

The chapter runs the ladder in three blocks on leaves 7-8, and it never says "our model is good" without first saying what each alternative cannot do:

> Section 4.2 reveals that Olympic medal data typically demand integer-valued predictions that sum to a known total, often exhibit overdispersion and heavy tails, and require partial pooling … deep learning and machine learning methods (e.g., RNN and two-stage RF) are clearly unsuitable, as they fail to enforce the fixed-total and discrete conditions without special modifications.

> Statistical approaches such as Negative Binomial and Poisson are also inadequate due to their poor handling of long-tailed data. While a standard Bayesian Multinomial Model can effectively model such data, it does not satisfy our Country Heterogeneity Assumption, as it assumes a shared 𝜷 across all countries, which is incompatible with our framework.

> The Bayesian Hierarchical Dirichlet–Multinomial (BHDM) model addresses these challenges by combining a Dirichlet–Multinomial likelihood, which enforces both discreteness and sum constraints, with hierarchical priors on regression parameters, enabling each country to have its own coefficient vector.

Why it is reusable: the last sentence is a mapping, not a claim. `Dirichlet–Multinomial likelihood` answers `discreteness and sum`, `hierarchical priors` answers `own coefficient vector`, and the reader can check the model against the two failures that survived the ladder without holding four adjectives in memory. The move also pre-empts the obvious reviewer question - why not XGBoost, which the same paper scores second in Table 2 - because the rejection is about the target's support, not about accuracy.

Why it fails here, in a way worth copying the fix of: the ladder sits in one paragraph that opens by citing the paper's own earlier section (`Section 4.2 reveals that …`) instead of deriving the requirement, no rival is cited or fitted, and the four requirements are stated once as adjectives and never referred to again by number - so the `two-stage RF` rejection cannot be checked against the `RF Two-stage` column that appears two pages later, and the `overdispersion and heavy tails` requirement is the only one of the four that no equation in the chapter uses. A reusable version labels the requirements (R1 integer support, R2 known total, R3 overdispersion, R4 cross-country heterogeneity), names the rival that breaks each, and keeps the same labels on the model's components.
