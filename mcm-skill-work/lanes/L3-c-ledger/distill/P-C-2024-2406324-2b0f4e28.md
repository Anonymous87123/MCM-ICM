## 5. Reusable writing action

**Reject your own first functional form with a boundary case, then name the replacement function and say what it amplifies.**

The chapter could have written the mapping from scoring rate to game score as a straight line and moved on. Instead it states the linear form as flawed, defends that with two concrete scorelines that pull in opposite directions, and only then introduces the sigmoid - naming the mechanism (`amplify`) rather than the shape:

> For example, previous research has pointed out that a 100% scoring rate to win a game should boost momentum slightly more than a 67% rate, such as leading 40:30 then winning one more point (Weinberg & Jackson, 1989). However, losing the game with a 33% rate, although still only differing by 33% from 67%, represents a huge change in momentum. Therefore, we introduced sigmoid function instead of the linear relationship between game score and scoring rate, to greatly amplify the impact on momentum between winning and losing a game.

Why it works: the two examples are 33 points apart in both directions, so a linear map is forced to give them the same weight, and the reader sees the failure without needing the algebra. The citation is placed on the *weaker* of the two claims (that 100% beats 67% only slightly) and the stronger one - the 33% loss being a huge change - is left uncited, so the argument rests on the reader's own experience of the scoreboard rather than on the literature.

How to reuse: pick the two cases your baseline model cannot tell apart, state both in the domain's own units, then introduce the replacement by the *behaviour* you need from it (`amplify`, `saturate`, `decay`) rather than by its name - and let the figure caption carry the name afterwards, which is what this chapter does one block later with `Figure 4: Comparison of the linear and sigmoid relationship between scoring rate and game score.`

The chapter's second-order version of the same move is worth copying too: after the sigmoid is accepted, it does not revise the boundary claim, it reuses the identical weight vector at point level - `To keep a sense of continuity, we set the weights for point scores to be the same as the weights for game scores, namely ωi = wi.` One decision, applied twice at two scales, and the reader is told which decision was reused.
