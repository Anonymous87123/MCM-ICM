## 5. Reusable writing action

**Action: give each derivation the same skeleton sentence, and make the skeleton enumerate the terms of the formula it introduces - one sentence per gate, three parts per sentence.**

The chapter derives two gates, and it derives them with one frame, printed almost word for word twice:

> which can be described as an addition of two parts. The first part is the overlap of oblivion gate output ft and long-term memory output from the previous layer Ct−1, and the second part is the information purified from the current status information.

> It can be described as a multiplication of two parts, the first part is an activation process just as the same as the oblivion gate, and the second part is the information purified from the current long-term memory output (Ct).

Why it works: the reader has just seen `Ct = ft ∗Ct−1 + it ∗˜Ct`. The frame does three jobs at once - it names the operation (`addition`, `multiplication`), it breaks the right-hand side into exactly the two terms that print there, and it gives each term a physical name a modelling reader can carry away (`the overlap of oblivion gate output ... and long-term memory output from the previous layer`, `the information purified from the current status information`). The formula is never left as notation; and because the two sentences are parallel, the second gate costs the reader no new parsing - they only have to notice that the operation changed.

Where it breaks, and the transferable lesson: the skeleton is reused so literally that the second sentence keeps the first's `the second part is the information purified from the current ...` clause, which is now describing a different quantity with the same words. The frame earns its repetition in the first half of each sentence (`the first part is ...`) and loses its information in the second half. And the two sentences are the only place the chapter says what the operations *are* - the formulas they gloss print in Figures 5 and 6 as pictures, so a reader who cannot see the figures gets the prose and no symbols, and a reader who cannot read the prose gets symbols with no names. Copy the frame; do not copy the habit of letting the frame carry terminology the text layer never defines.
