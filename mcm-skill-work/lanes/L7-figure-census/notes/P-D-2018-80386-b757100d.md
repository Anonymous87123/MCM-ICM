## Templates this paper hands you

**Which section takes which exhibit.** This paper's answer is a chain, not a division:
the world map of the input variable comes first (5.3.1), then the technique's plots
(5.3.2: four country scatters), then the combined discriminant picture (5.3.3), and the
sensitivity plot closes the paper alone in section 7. The reusable rule: put the map of
the RAW input ahead of any derived chart, because the four country scatters cannot be
read without the Gini map that produced two of their axes. And note where the two Excel
forecast charts land - in the `.2` sub-subsection of the rollout section, with their data
tables pasted inside the frame, so an audience can check the projection without a table
of their own.

**When three dimensions appear.** Never, in thirteen figures - and this paper needed one
the least because its whole argument is classification on TWO axes. The four country
plots print as flat scatter plus an ellipse outline, which is a 2-D decision boundary
drawn honestly; a 3-D surface over the same two variables would have hidden the boundary
the caption is about. The only figure that fakes depth is Figure 7, whose choropleth
reads as a continuous ramp but is keyed as eight discrete bands - a reminder that a
colour ramp implies interpolation the data does not have.

**Table type against data scale.** Two grid shapes cover this paper: a wide symbol table
(three columns, eighteen rows, unit in a real column) for the model's front matter, and a
narrow three-column result grid (four rows) for the answer. Both are horizontal-only with
zebra banding, so the format survives photocopying and the alternating band does the
row-tracking that vertical rules would otherwise do. Worth copying: keep the parameter
table FULL measure and the result grid at 80 per cent, because the parameter table is
read down and the result grid is read across. Not worth copying: the four unmeasured
grids that carry three heavy strokes and zebra but no verticals, which is why they read
as prose blocks rather than tables in any automated pass.

**Caption template.** `<Label><number>: The <quantity> of <object>` with the number glued
to the label and a colon after it - "Figure8: The population distribution of Australia",
"Table1: The needs of the charging piles and charging stations" - seven words at the
median, sixteen at the longest, one sentence, no verb, no unit, no condition, no
conclusion. The template is so fixed that it fails twice: "Figure6: Data analysis of the
figure X." still carries the placeholder X, and Figure 3/4's two labels were merged onto
one printed line because neither had artwork to stand over. Keep the geography qualifier
last; it is the only slot that changes between near-identical panels.

**Context-weld template.** This paper brackets a figure on BOTH sides and never numbers
it: a lead-in that states the question ("According to the data given by the World
Regional Wealth Statistics Bureau, we can get the two types of countries' Gini
coefficient."), then the artwork, then a reading sentence that names the encoding ("The
red cross in the figure is not in the 50% circle, and the blue point have the opposite
meaning"). The habit to take: when four panels are the same drawing with different data,
replace the legend with a prose sentence under the caption and put a country header above
the pair ("Unbalanced country:" / "Balanced Country：") - it costs two words per panel and
it survives the merge of the two caption fragments onto one line. The habit to avoid:
referring to everything as "the figure above" or "the image below" and never once naming
an exhibit by number except the first one.
