## Templates this paper hands you

**Which section takes which exhibit.** The split is by FIDELITY, not by subsection: the 2D
half spends one cheap vector drawing per idea (a sunburst for the factors, a plan view for the
assumption, one transformation diagram, two graph drawings, one node scatter, one flowchart)
and the 3D half spends one rendered field per claim (a surface pair for the danger index, a
heat-map grid for the evacuation time, a density grid and a zoom render for the bottlenecks).
What to copy: the divide between "draw the construction" and "render the field". Schematics
cost nothing to read and are used wherever a definition is being made; the moment the exhibit
has to carry a number per location, the paper pays for a colour field.

**When three dimensions are worth drawing.** Four times out of fifteen, and once wrongly: the
floor stack of Figure 3 (the model's whole trick, motion turned from stairs to flat), the
surface pair of Figure 9 (a quantity over the floor plate), the render of Figure 15 (a
screenshot the simulation software hands over), and the extruded pie of Figure 7 - which its
own twin Figure 8 prints flat, so the extrusion is provably decoration. What to copy: extrude
only when the object exists in 3-D; when the same chart is drawn twice with different data, do
not let the second one keep the perspective.

**Table type against data size.** The one table that got a grid is the notation list: 2
columns, 20 rows, three rules, no verticals, and a blank unruled row standing in for a
sub-header. The three tables that were typeset as prose are the small numeric ones (4 to 12
stub rows, 2 to 3 columns) - below some size threshold these authors simply set the values in
running text and let the caption do the identifying. The reverse of the usual advice: at small
sizes the caption is doing the work a grid would do.

**Caption template.** `Figure n: <noun phrase>` - three to fifteen words, one sentence, no
finite verb, no unit, no variable, no condition, no conclusion. Panels are never named in the
caption; Part(I)/Part(II) are printed on the art and the ordinals are counted in the prose
("the third part of Figure 10"). Beware the copy: two figures here share one caption verbatim
and two tables share another, so a caption is a statement of intent, not a description of the
marks.

**Context connectives.** (1) Bare imperative as its own sentence after a setup clause - "just
as the following figure illustrates. See Figure 7." (2) Inline pointer when the exhibit is the
grammatical subject - "The specific factors we consider are shown in Figure 1." (3)
Parenthetical for a detail inside a larger drawing - "(The four blue circle in Figure 5
respectively represent one of the four frequently-used exits.)" (4) Read-back that restates a
trend and never a number - "We can clearly see that 90% of the evacuees leave the building
from Exit #1, making this exit extremely overloaded." (5) Sweep read-back naming the panel
ordinal - "The third part of Figure 10 shows that the time of evacuation is not that stable
when ... is smaller than 0.5."
