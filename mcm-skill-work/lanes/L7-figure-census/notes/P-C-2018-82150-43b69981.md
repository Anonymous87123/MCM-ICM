## Templates this paper hands you

**Which section takes which exhibit.** Four-state comparisons go in pairs: a profile
section draws the chart (Figure 4-1/4-2 stacked shares, 4-3 per-capita lines, 4-5
structure columns), and the moment the paper needs an exact number it stops drawing
and prints a grid instead (Table 4-1 when the prose needs the TEP/TEC totals). A
ranking section then runs tables first and the map last: Table 4-2, Table 4-3, then
Figure 4-10's choropleth as the closing picture. When the paper moves from describing
to solving, it opens the section with a flowchart (Figure 5-1) and closes the search
results with a convergence curve (Figure 5-2). Appendix: grids only, never a chart.

**When three dimensions appear.** Never. Fourteen figure rows and not one uses a
perspective, a surface or a third axis; where two variables have to be shown against
a third the paper stacks them in 2-D (100 % stacked columns, grouped columns) rather
than lifting the plot. The template to take away is the opposite of the tempting one:
a paper with an index on one side and a per-capita trend on the other still draws
flat.

**Table type against data scale.** Two columns and two or three rows: parameter or
denotation grid, title bar only, no header row (Table 3-1). Five columns and five
rows: one header row, four state rows, three horizontals (Tables 4-2, 4-3, 4-4).
Seven columns and five rows: header cells wrap over three lines and the grid grows
past the measure (Tables 4-5, 4-6). The jump in structure happens only at the
grouping: a twenty-one-row grid is the first one that needs a merged sector cell and
group separators, and it is the only one that gets them.

**Caption template.** Number, colon, lower-case noun phrase, no verb, no period:
"Figure 4-6: The evolution of TEC in five sector from 1979 to 2009". Figures get the
caption below, tables above, and the appendix drops the number and keeps the label
("Table: The proportion of electricity origins"). Nothing in the caption states the
unit - the unit is a separate right-aligned line above the grid ("Unit: Percent") or
is left in the prose.

**Context-weld template.** Lead with a sentence that ends in a colon and names the
thing, not the artwork: "The prediction charts are as follows:", "The algorithm flow
is as follows:". Then the exhibit. Then one interpretive sentence that says what the
reader should see ("California excels in all respects and ranks first."). Where the
paper wants the number in the prose it does not caption the artwork at all - it
writes "(refer to figure 4-10)" after the value it just computed. The pattern worth
copying is the pairing rule: a deictic lead and a one-sentence reading, never a
caption that argues.
