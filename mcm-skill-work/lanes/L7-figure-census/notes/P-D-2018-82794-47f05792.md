## Templates this paper hands you

**Which section takes which exhibit.** The model half shows structure, the results half
shows numbers. Sections 4.1, 4.2 and 4.3 carry four exhibits, three of them drawings of the
network itself - a county-of-cliques graph (Figure 1), a mind map of the subsections
(Figure 2), a hand-drawn county blob map (Figure 4) - and the fourth a doughnut of
station counts (Figure 3) that wandered in from the numbers chapter. The entire Ireland
chapter 6.2 carries seven figures and three tables, one chart per claim. The
reusable rule: a schematic per model component in the model section, then a chart per
computed quantity in the results section, with the notation table sitting immediately
after the sentence that promises it. The part not to copy: Figure 3 lands between the
second and third bullets of the list that introduces Figure 4, so the one exhibit that
belongs to the numbers chapter is interleaved with the one that belongs to the model
chapter.

**When three dimensions appear.** Never, and neither does a contour. Twenty-one exhibits,
one colourbar in the whole document (Figure 10's 0-1400 marker ramp, floated inside the
map), zero 3-D surfaces. The quantitative repertoire is a line, a diverging bar and a
graduated-symbol map - which is the correct set for a paper whose variables are all
saturation curves, shares and pin counts. Anything the paper could not express as a curve
of permeability against time, it drew as a picture of a network instead.

**Table type against data scale.** Two recipes, chosen by the shape of the columns rather
than by taste. Plain two-dimensional arrays get three rules and nothing else - top,
header, bottom, no verticals - which is Tables 1, 3 and 4, at 3x4 and 5x2, all inside
the measure. Nested classifications get the full closed box: Tables 2 and 5 are the same
table in two countries, and both need merged cells on two axes ('type' across two
columns, 'destination charging stations' across the same two, 'supercharging stations'
down two rows), a unit inside the header ('number(million)' / 'number(thousand)') and
decimal-aligned values. That package - box + merges + header unit + decimal alignment -
appears exactly twice and never on a flat array. Worth copying: the unit goes in the
header and the decimals line up. Not worth copying: two different tables carrying the
identical caption 'the number of charging stations'.

**Caption template.** `<Label> <number>: <lower-case noun phrase>` - "Figure 4: the
outer-layer Network", "Table 3: the distribution of supercharging stations" - three to
eighteen words, one sentence, voice `none` on all twenty-one, no unit, no variable, no
condition, no conclusion, printed below the artwork for figures and above the grid for
tables. The table side has one twist worth keeping in mind: Table 1's caption prints
BETWEEN the doubled top rule and the header row, so the extracted header line reads
'Symbol  Table 1: Notations / Description' and a naive reader sees the caption as a
column heading. Two pairs are welded - Figures 11/12 on one line, Figures 13/14 on one
line with the second caption's words spilling onto the next line - which is the
side-by-side layout leaking into the caption layer.

**Context-weld template.** 'As shown in Figure N' is the paper's default: nine of the
twenty-one rows are introduced that way, and the pointer usually sits one page before the
artwork (Figures 4, 13, 15), which is a habit of floating the figure to the next page
rather than of writing. Three exhibits are never named at all (Figures 3, 10, 12) and
seven are introduced by an unlabelled clause that carries the modelling step instead of
the figure ('Apply the ratio of vehicle possession to equation 4 to get the number of
distribution for each city:'). The habit worth taking: the clause states what the figure
computes, so the reader knows what to look for. The habit to avoid: Figure 8 is the only
row in the paper followed by a reading sentence, and even that one reads the coordinate
labels rather than the curves.
