## When the paper goes three-dimensional

Once, in Figure 7 (`three_d = surface-mesh`, `colorbar = true`), and the condition is the
shape of the data rather than the topic: the claim is that a course section is a surface
(a slope plus its direction), so the authors render a terrain ribbon with the four section
types standing on it. The moment the same information is needed per course, in section 6,
they abandon depth entirely and use a flat elevation profile with colour-banded segments
and printed gradients (Figures 12, 16, 19). Rule to carry: 3-D appears as a one-off
geometry explainer, never as a result and never as a panel of a composite; the flat
colour-banded profile is the substitute.

## Table shape vs data size

- 6 rows x 2 cols (Table 1): the rider-type list, the widest text table, no numbers.
- 7 rows x 3 cols and 6 rows x 4 cols (Tables 2, 3): indicator lists; the second adds a
  formula column, which is why its cells arrive as loose maths fragments.
- 2 rows x 7 cols and 2 rows x 6 cols (Tables 4, 5) and 2 rows x 3 cols (Table 6): the
  parameter carriers. One data row per course, one rider for the control variate.
- 11 rows x 6 cols and 9 rows x 6 cols (Tables 7, 10): the per-section calculation dumps,
  one row per course section (ten and eight sections) with units in the header.
- 6 rows x 5 cols (Table 13) and 5 rows x 4 cols (Table 14): the sensitivity grids; only
  the last one is half width, because its stub column holds strategy names, not numbers.
- Pattern: table height is set by how many rows the model consumes, not by how much the
  authors want to show. Six of the fourteen tables carry one data row, and those six are
  the ones whose unit goes inside the cell; the two tall tables carry units in the header
  because their cells are pure numbers.
- No table spans a page or continues onto the next, and no table is wider than the text
  block: `width` is full on thirteen of fourteen.

## Caption templates

- Numbered figure: `Figure N: <noun phrase>`, 5 to 17 words, one sentence on all 20 rows,
  `caption.voice = none` throughout. No verbs at all: the captions are labels, and what
  the reader should take away is written in the body paragraph above.
- Numbered table: `Table N: <noun phrase>`, 4 to 10 words, same rule, `position = above`.
- Units: only Table 14 puts a unit in the caption (`(%)`); the rest either leave it to the
  header row or write it inside the cells.
- Symbol definitions inside the caption: Figure 3 is the only one, with two parentheticals
  (`(t − maintain in the ﬁgure)`, `(P in the ﬁgure)`) that map the drawing's letters onto
  the model's symbols.
- Final punctuation is not normalised: fourteen figure captions end with a stop, six do
  not (Figs 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 14), and no table caption does.
- Recycling is the diagnostic here: identical caption strings mark the repeated block.
  `Course elevation proﬁle.` (Figs 12, 16, 19), `Diagram of power, speed, elevation and
  distance.` (Figs 13, 17, 20), `Course information` (Tables 5, 8, 11), `Course
  environmental factors` (Tables 6, 9, 12), and Tables 2 and 3 share one caption although
  their columns differ.

## Connector-sentence templates

- Lead, lifted clause (4 figure rows + 3 table rows): the pointer is the tail of a running
  sentence, never a sentence of its own - `... and the ﬁtting results are shown in
  Figure 5.`, `... this rider's information is given in Table 4.`, `... the results can be
  obtained(Table 7).` (parenthesis glued to the preceding word).
- Lead, as-shown (1 row): `The inﬂuence of weather and environment on the race is complex,
  as is shown in Figure 6:` - the only lead that ends in a colon, announcing a list of
  factors rather than a plot.
- Lead, wrapped across a line break (1 row): Fig 7's clause is split as `... as is` /
  `shown in Figure 7.`, so any quote of it has to be assembled from two raw lines.
- Lead, verb-first (1 row): `The obtained results are visualized in Figure 13.` and the
  terse `..., we get Figure 14.` - the paper writes the second pointer as if continuing
  the first, which is why Figure 14's first page and Figure 13's are the same.
- No lead at all (27 of 35 rows): the exhibit is reached through its caption or through a
  deictic `as follows:`. The paper never opens a sentence with `Figure N shows`.
- Trail: none. Not one of the 35 rows has a sentence after the graphic that reads it back;
  every interpretation of a figure sits in the body paragraph above the caption or inside
  the caption itself. `read_sentences = 1` everywhere, and no `trail` is recorded.
- Citation counts: only two exhibits are cited twice (Fig 6, one page before its caption;
  Table 7, one page before), so the paper's habit is to introduce an exhibit in the same
  breath as printing it.
