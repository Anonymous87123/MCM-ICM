## Templates this paper hands you

**Which section takes which exhibit.** Two construction pictures open the paper and
nothing else is a mere illustration: the technology route closes 1.2 before any
mathematics exists, the piecewise index curve closes 4.1.1, the two-panel schematic
opens 4.1.2 with the arrow carrying the argument, and the twelve-indicator mind map
opens 4.2.1 and is then re-listed as bullet points for three pages. After that every
picture is a computed quantity, one per step: the four dendrograms close 4.3's
clustering, the classification bands and the FSI comparison stay in 4.3, the two
Somali charts open 5.1, the normality panels close it, the coupling fit closes 6.2,
the forecast closes 6.3, and the confidence band closes 9. The reusable rule is that a
construction diagram sits at the head of the section that needs it and a result chart
at the tail of the section that produced it - so a reader can tell, from position
alone, whether the picture is a definition or a measurement. What not to copy: Fig.10
and Fig.11 print in the wrong order, so the coupling fit arrives two pages after the
comparison chart that is supposed to justify it.

**When three dimensions appear.** Never, in 23 pages and thirteen charts - and this
paper is the cleanest case in the lane for the alternative: it draws depth with
gradient fills and drop shadows (the shaded probability tails of Fig.3, the shaded
globe of Fig.4, the gradient bars of Fig.7) and encodes value with the same device
(the yellow-to-purple ramps of Fig.6 and Fig.7). One vocabulary, two jobs, and the
reader cannot tell which is decoration and which is data. Copy the flatness; do not
copy the colour gradient as a value channel unless you key it, because only Fig.6
does, and it keys it with a legend printed below the frame whose first word is
truncated to 'impregnabl'.

**Table type against data scale.** Three grades and a clean mapping this time. Two
columns and eight rows of definitions get three-rule booktabs with the caption above
and no verticals (Table 1), even though that grid then splits across the p4/p5 fold
with no repeated header. Four numbers get three rules and five columns (Table 4) -
the smallest exhibit in the paper. Only a real two-way data table gets a full box:
Table 2's five columns and twelve rows with every internal rule drawn, a merged group
name down four rows in one column while the group weight merges down the same four in
another, and categorical shading that separates the two weight scales. What is worth
copying: the four-decimal alignment of every weight, and the caption above the grid
that names the unit of the table's meaning ('Weight values of the twelve evaluation
indicators and three comprehensive indexes'). What is not: the twelfth indicator's
weight sits in the same column as the first, so a reader scanning the peach column
reads twelve numbers with no row labels to hold them.

**Caption template.** `Fig.<n> <noun phrase>` with NO space after the abbreviation and
no colon - "Fig.1 Technology route for the creation of our paper.", "Fig.6
Classification standards and their respective critical points of verifying a
country's fragility" - and for tables `Table <n> <noun phrase>` with no colon either
("Table 1 Notations"). Thirteen figures: every caption below, every caption one to
three sentences, 3 to 40 words, no unit, no stated conclusion, and only eight naming a
variable at all. The two exceptions are worth keeping: where a chart has no legend,
the caption becomes the legend and says so in words ("The blue line is made by the
method of cubic spline, and the black dotted line is a fitting curve"), and where a
figure is a panel grid, the caption carries the panel key - which is exactly where
this paper fails, keying panels "(a), (b), (c), (b)" for a grid lettered (a) to (d).
Retype nothing: quote the caption from the text layer, because Fig.7's ends "from FCI
." with the space its dropped equation glyph left behind, and Fig.11's captions the
wrong country two pages later.

**Context-weld template.** Twelve of sixteen exhibits are pointed at by number, and
the welding sentence always comes after the paragraph that needs the picture, never
before it: "As the Fig.3 shows, the annual anomaly ...", "As is vividly shown in
Fig.4, in the context of global warming ...", "As is shown in Fig.6, the
classification standards ... vary a little", "According to Fig.9, the daily average
temperature and precipitation scatterplots fit very well". Two of those run BACKWARD
(Fig.5 read on p13 from p12, Fig.11 read on p17 from p16), which is a habit, not an
error: the paper places a figure where it finishes drawing it and reads it on the next
page. Copy the sentence shape - `As <pointer>, <the finding>` and then the exhibit -
and copy the discipline of naming the number, since the four exhibits that are never
named (Fig.1, Fig.2, Fig.12, Fig.13) are exactly the four a reader cannot find again.
What not to copy: the pointer that names the wrong number ("As the Fig.3 shows" for
the Fig.2 curve) and the pointer that names the wrong country (Mexico's section read
as Kenya), because a pointer that is wrong is worse than no pointer at all.
