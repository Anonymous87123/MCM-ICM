## Templates this paper hands you

**Which section takes which exhibit.** The paper answers this by country, not by section
type: each modelling section ends by promising its picture, and the picture then prints
one or two pages later in the results half. The reusable part is the pairing rule - a
node cloud, a surface and a contour per country, in that order, so the reader sees where
the things are, how dense they get and where the density peaks before any number is
quoted. The part not to copy: the surface and the contour are printed as two numbered
figures with different extents (-120..-70 by 25..50 against -130..-60 by 20..55), so they
cannot be overlaid, and the contour carries no colourbar.

**When three dimensions appear.** Twice, and only as the middle step of the cloud/-
surface/contour triple. The conditions that make it legitimate here: the two horizontal
axes are geographic and shared with the sibling figure, the vertical axis is a real
quantity with printed ticks (0-20000-25000 US, 0-16000 Korea), and the projection is a
plain perspective mesh with grid walls rather than a shaded solid. What does not work is
that the surface half is the only quantified half - the contour that follows it drops the
scale - so a draft that copies the pair should put the colourbar on the contour, not rely
on the surface's wireframe.

**Table type against data scale.** Two shapes cover this paper and both are turned
sideways: a repeated `Location | Numbers` row pair for the ten-city list (4 rows x 6
columns, two stacked five-city blocks) and a `Year` header row for the model calibration
(5 rows x 7 columns, years 2012-2017 across the top). Both are closed boxes with rules on
every internal boundary and no zebra, both stop at 52-56 per cent of the measure, and
both put the caption below. Worth copying: keep the transposed layout for lists of
labelled values, because the label column then reads as a stub and the numbers line up.
Not worth copying: the five tables that print with no rules at all - they read as prose
and the census cannot see them.

**Caption template.** `<Label> <number>: <noun phrase>` with a space after the label and a
colon after the number - "Figure 5: Chargers Distribution Contour Figure in U.S.",
"Table 5: Number of Chargers in 10 Major South Korean Cities" - five to ten words, one
sentence, no verb, no unit, no condition, no conclusion, printed below with a 10.6 to
58.3 pt gap. It is the most consistently spelled caption set in this census, and the
failure mode that follows from its rigidity is worth knowing: Table 6's template broke
and the caption swallowed a body sentence ("... value of 0.375, the value of P0 should be
stable at 0.275."), and two captions name the wrong thing (Figure 2 says Salt Lake City
over Nashville coordinates, Figure 6 says "Connections" over a point cloud with no edges).

**Context-weld template.** The paper's joining sentence is a forecasting promise in front
of the exhibit and nothing behind it: "The charger connections can be seen in Figure 4.",
"Figure 6 shows the predicted superchargers location in South Korea.", "to find an
optimized solution(Table 6)." - label glued to number, no parentheses around the number,
no reading sentence after. That is why so many of its citations lead the exhibit by a
page. The habit to take: name the exhibit BEFORE it prints and put the reading in the
same sentence, so a reader who never turns the page still gets the claim. The habit to
avoid: never explaining the encoding - this paper's legends are absent nine times out of
twelve, and the two annotation rings on Figure 2 are never labelled anywhere.
