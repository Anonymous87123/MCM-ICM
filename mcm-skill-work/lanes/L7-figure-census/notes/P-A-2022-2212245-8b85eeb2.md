## When the paper goes three-dimensional

Never. `three_d = no` on all 21 figure rows and `colorbar = false` on all of them, and no
exhibit carries a perspective axis, a mesh floor or a shading ramp. The paper encodes the
two things depth is usually used for by other means: magnitude by colour bands and printed
values on the map and profile drawings (Figs 12, 13, 14), and multi-condition comparison by
stacking panels and twinning the axes (Figs 15, 16, 18, 19). Carrying rule: a 2022-style A
paper can be entirely 2-D and still show three variables at once by putting velocity on the
left axis and power on the right.

## Caption templates

- Numbered figure: `Figure N: <noun phrase>`, 4 to 12 words, one sentence on all 20 rows,
  `caption.voice = none` throughout: no caption states what the figure shows.
- Numbered table: `Table N: <noun phrase>`, 3 to 11 words, `position = above` on all seven.
- Case is not normalised: `Figure 19: detailed information` starts lower case and is as
  vague as a caption can be; `Table 5: Characteristic powers of sprinters` keeps a lower-case
  noun where Table 3 uses capitals for the same phrase.
- Reference markers are pasted into captions: `Figure 1: Fuji Speedway, Tokyo 2020 Olympic
  Game[3]` carries the citation inside the caption line, and the singular "Game" is the
  authors' slip.
- Caption recycling is the paper's worst habit and worth a rule: Figure 16 repeats Figure
  12's caption word for word over a completely different chart, Figure 13's caption says
  Female while the embedded map it labels says Men, and Figure 20's caption says individual
  time trial over a team-rotation drawing. Four of the twenty captions therefore misdescribe
  their artwork, and the in-plot titles are more accurate than the printed captions.
- The four composite rows have only (a)/(b) letters for three elements, or no letters at all
  (Figure 10, Figure 18), so the caption is never a reliable guide to how many panels exist.
