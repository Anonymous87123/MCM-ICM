## Templates this paper hands you

**Which section takes which exhibit.** Divide the design into one block per question and
open each block with a map, then let the counts follow the map rather than lead it. This
paper does exactly that in 4.1: 4.1.1 draws the concept (pixel mosaic plus hexagon
callout), 4.1.2 answers it (a US map, a grid, then a second map and grid pair for the
interstate case). The reusable rule: the conceptual figure lands in the `.1` subsection and
the measured figures in the `.2`, never the other way round, and the section that only
reports numbers gets the grid alone with no artwork.

**When three dimensions appear.** Never, in twenty-six figures - and the paper still ends
up with an explicit spatial argument, because all of it is carried by maps in plan view
plus a bar pair with independent y axes. If a draft is tempted to reach for a 3-D surface
to show "density over area", this paper is the counter-example: a flat choropleth plus a
labelled inset does the job and survives printing.

**Table type against data scale.** Four columns is the ceiling for a measurement grid in
the body: stub, then one column per channel, then a total. The moment the paper needs more
than four it switches to a SCENARIO header (Table 4.5's 30/50/100 per cent) rather than
adding rows. Only the appendix grows tall, and only because it prints a raw ordered list -
thirty-one rows and sixty-two entries - where a body grid never exceeds five. Unit
strategy is uniform and worth copying: keep the unit inside every cell as a word, never in
the header, so each cell reads on its own if the table is quoted out of context.

**Caption template.** `<Label> <number> The <quantity> of <object> <locative phrase>` -
"Table 4.4 The number of different charging stations in Ireland", "Figure 4.6 The location
of charging stations in the US". Ten words at the median, no verb, no unit, no conclusion,
and the locative phrase is doing all the work: it is what tells a reader which of the six
near-identical maps they are looking at. When a figure is a process rather than a count,
the template loosens to a noun phrase with one qualifier - "The change of the charging
station network construction" - which is why those captions run to fifteen words.

**Context-weld template.** The paper's joining sentence is almost always a colon-led
promise followed by the object's name in the caption: "the result is shown below:",
"the results are shown in the following Table 4.4 and Figure 4.7", "The specific steps of
the model are as follows:". Two habits are worth taking: name BOTH exhibits when a number
and a picture arrive together, and put the reading sentence AFTER the map ("we can clearly
see..."), never before it. The one habit not to take is the appendix: it promises Appendix
3 twelve pages before printing it, which is a cross-reference a reader cannot follow.
