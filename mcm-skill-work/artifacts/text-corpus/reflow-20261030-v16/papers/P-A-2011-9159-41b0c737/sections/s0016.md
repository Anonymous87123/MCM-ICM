5 Application in 3D Case

Now we move our study to a more reasonable and complex case involving the third dimension of the halfpipe. In fact, the halfpipe does not lie horizontally, but is oriented downhill with a slope angle ϕ. In this way, the sliding is not a pure energy loss process, but can gain energy from the gravity. Therefore, the third dimension will alter the velocity of the snowboarder, which will bring changes to a∗and b∗.

We claim that a similar hanging height above the deck on each side of the halfpipe is a sign for good ϕ. The reason is as the follows. In a round of snowboard contest, the player will perform 6-7 tricks on both sides of the halfpipe. Thus small ϕ will result in the condition that the player never takes air. Also, too large ϕ will lead to higher and higher hanging height, which is dangerous for the player. Therefore, we look for ϕ∗that makes the same hang height above the deck on both sides. The initial hang height above the ground hi is still assumed to be 9.0, cause the halfpipe is symmetric, ϕ∗should make the ﬁnal height above the ground hf equals to 9.0m. We calculate the hf −ϕ relationship in Fig. 19, ﬁt the data by using cubic spline interpolation and ﬁnally get the point intersected by the hf −ϕ curve and the line hf = 9.0m. Thus ϕ∗is captured, which is 17.1o.

The corresponding (a∗, b∗) for ϕ∗= 17.1o is (4.42m, 5.4m).
