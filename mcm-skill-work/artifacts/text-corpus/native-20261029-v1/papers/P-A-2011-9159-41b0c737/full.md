In this paper, we study the shape of a snowboard course and its influential factors

<!-- b000005 | prose | pages [1] -->
under energetic view. The main idea is to measure the "vertical air" by final energy. Before calculation, several assumptions are made for simplicity.

<!-- b000006 | prose | pages [1] -->
 The snowboarder is treated as a mass point;

<!-- b000007 | prose | pages [1] -->
 The halfpipe is regarded as a perfectly rigid body;

<!-- b000008 | prose | pages [1] -->
 Sliding friction, collision and air resistance are introduced step by step, while

<!-- b000009 | prose | pages [1] -->
other sources of energy loss are ignored.

<!-- b000010 | prose | pages [1] -->
With these assumptions, we build our basic model, which is simple but sufficient

<!-- b000011 | prose | pages [1] -->
to offer a fundamental idea and reveal essential interactions among different factors.

<!-- b000012 | prose | pages [1] -->
We make force analysis, set up kinematical equations and obtain the general form of final mechanical energy analytically.

<!-- b000013 | prose | pages [1] -->
After that, two specific types of transition curve, quarter circle and quarter ellipse,

<!-- b000014 | prose | pages [1] -->
are introduced to calculate the optimal design. Various factors are systematically

<!-- b000015 | prose | pages [1] -->
analyzed in both models. In quarter-circle model, we obtain analytical solutions and find the best radius R* of the circle. While in quarter-ellipse model, we use numerical

<!-- b000016 | prose | pages [1] -->
simulation to get the optimal semi-major axis a* and semi-minor axis b*. Then we

<!-- b000017 | prose | pages [1] -->
further develop the 2D quarter-ellipse model into a more actual and complex case,

<!-- b000018 | prose | pages [1] -->
involving the third dimension of the halfpipe. Through numerical computation, we find the most suitable slope angle of the halfpipe.

<!-- b000019 | prose | pages [1] -->
Next, by introducing the sensitivity coefficient into these models, the sensitivities

<!-- b000020 | prose | pages [1] -->
of different variables are analyzed both qualitatively and quantitatively. It helps us to

<!-- b000021 | prose | pages [1] -->
compare the significance of these variables in building a best halfpipe.

<!-- b000022 | prose | pages [1] -->
At last, the tailors and tradeoffs to develop a "practical" course are discussed. To

<!-- b000023 | prose | pages [1] -->
further optimize the shape of the halfpipe, we take various requirements into

<!-- b000024 | prose | pages [1] -->
consideration, including construction difficulty, players' safety and their performance.

<!-- b000025 | prose | pages [1] -->
After adjustment, our result becomes more practical.

<!-- b000026 | prose | pages [1] -->
In conclusion, through systematic and comprehensive analysis of various factors,

<!-- b000027 | prose | pages [1] -->
we find a reasonable shape of the snowboard course, which fits the real data well.

<!-- b000156 | prose | pages [5] -->
Snowboarding is an adventurous and ex-

<!-- b000157 | prose | pages [5] -->
citing sport that has been contested at the

<!-- b000158 | prose | pages [5] -->
Winter Olympic Games since 1998.

<!-- b000159 | prose | pages [5] -->
Till

<!-- b000160 | prose | pages [5] -->
now, the events are held in three speciali-

<!-- b000161 | prose | pages [5] -->
ties: parallel giant slalom, snowboard cross

<!-- b000162 | prose | pages [5] -->
and halfpipe.[2] What we are interested here

<!-- b000163 | prose | pages [5] -->
is the halfpipe, in which competitors perform

<!-- b000164 | prose | pages [5] -->
tricks while going from one side of a ditch to

<!-- b000165 | prose | pages [5] -->

<!-- b000166 | prose | pages [5] -->
For both ornamental and safety

<!-- b000167 | prose | pages [5] -->
purpose, the shape of the halfpipe is of vital importance.

<!-- b000168 | prose | pages [5] -->
In its most basic form, a halfpipe consists of two concave ramps (or quarter-

<!-- b000169 | prose | pages [5] -->
pipes), topped by copings and decks, facing each other across a transition and an

<!-- b000170 | prose | pages [5] -->
extended ﬂat ground (the ﬂat bottom) added between the quarterpipes. For snow-

<!-- b000171 | prose | pages [5] -->
boarding, the plane of the transition is oriented downhill at a slight grade to allow

<!-- b000172 | prose | pages [5] -->
riders to use gravity to develop speed and facilitate drainage of melt.

<!-- b000173 | prose | pages [5] -->
There are three main tasks in this paper as follows:

<!-- b000174 | prose | pages [5] -->
• Determine the shape of a snowboard course which can generate the highest

<!-- b000175 | prose | pages [5] -->
jump above the edge of the halfpipe.

<!-- b000176 | prose | pages [5] -->
• Optimize the shape to satisfy other requirements of the tricks.

<!-- b000177 | prose | pages [5] -->
• In practical, what properties of the halfpipe should be abandoned.

<!-- b000178 | prose | pages [5] -->
We analyze the problem from the viewpoint of energy translation. The compe-

<!-- b000179 | prose | pages [5] -->
tition between the inﬂux from gravitational potential energy and the loss caused

<!-- b000180 | prose | pages [5] -->
by friction and collision determines the process of sliding. To study the energy

<!-- b000181 | prose | pages [5] -->
change in this process, we ﬁrstly consider about the 2D case. We build a simple

<!-- b000182 | prose | pages [5] -->
quarter circle-type model to study what factors involve in and what role they play.

<!-- b000183 | prose | pages [5] -->
Next the sliding process is further simulated by introducing an elliptic-type trail.

<!-- b000184 | prose | pages [5] -->
The energy loss is calculated numerically to obtain an ideal shape for 2D trail.

<!-- b000185 | prose | pages [5] -->
Then we take the third dimension into consideration. We work out the slope an-

<!-- b000186 | prose | pages [5] -->
gle by balancing the energy gaining and losing in new 3D model, which is developed

<!-- b000187 | prose | pages [5] -->
from former 2D case.

6.1 Safety Analysis

<!-- b000611 | prose | pages [20] -->
No one wants to get hurt in games. The safety requirements should always be

<!-- b000612 | prose | pages [20] -->
put in the ﬁrst place. Take the design of edge angle as an example. In theoretical

<!-- b000613 | prose | pages [20] -->
halfpipe the slope angle near edges, named as θe, should be close to 90o. However,

<!-- b000614 | prose | pages [20] -->
such large θe means less holding force from halfpipe to player, which will increase

<!-- b000615 | prose | pages [20] -->
the possibility for player to ﬂy away the track too early. At the same time, when

<!-- b000616 | prose | pages [20] -->
the player falls onto the surface of the halfpipes edge from air, the shock is in

<!-- b000617 | prose | pages [20] -->
proportional to cos θ. So small θe may increase the shock, hurting players or making

<!-- b000618 | prose | pages [20] -->
them lose their balance. In conclusion, θe should neither be too large nor too small.

<!-- b000619 | prose | pages [20] -->
Usually this is set from 83o to 88o [3].

<!-- b000620 | prose | pages [20] -->
The slope angle of the halfpipe, ϕ, is another factors related to safety. Larger

<!-- b000621 | prose | pages [20] -->
ϕ means higher jump but also higher danger. Therefore, while adjusting ϕ in our

<!-- b000622 | prose | pages [20] -->
design, we could only slightly increase it from the theoretical value 17.1o to about

<!-- b000623 | prose | pages [20] -->
17.5o. This is also the actual slope angle used in regular game nowadays.

<!-- b000624 | prose | pages [20] -->
Besides, to minimize the energy loss caused by friction, the bottom part should

<!-- b000625 | prose | pages [20] -->
be abandoned (in other words, a should equals w). However, we maintain this part,

<!-- b000626 | prose | pages [20] -->
to give the player more time to prepare the next jump.

Conclusion

<!-- b000677 | prose | pages [22] -->
In this paper, we study the shape of a snowboard course and its inﬂuential

<!-- b000678 | prose | pages [22] -->
factors under energetic view. The main idea is to measure the ”vertical air” by

<!-- b000679 | prose | pages [22] -->
ﬁnal energy. After making several assumptions, we build our basic model, which

<!-- b000680 | prose | pages [22] -->
is simple but suﬃcient to oﬀer a fundamental idea and reveal essential interactions

<!-- b000681 | prose | pages [22] -->
among diﬀerent factors. We make force analysis, set up kinematical equations and

<!-- b000682 | prose | pages [22] -->
obtain the general form of ﬁnal mechanical energy analytically.

<!-- b000683 | prose | pages [22] -->
After that, two speciﬁc types of transition curve are introduced to calculate the

<!-- b000684 | prose | pages [22] -->
optimal design. In quarter-circle model, we obtain analytical solutions and ﬁnd

<!-- b000685 | prose | pages [22] -->
the best radius R∗of the circle. While in quarter-ellipse model, we use numerical

<!-- b000686 | prose | pages [22] -->
simulation to get the optimal semi-major axis a∗and semi-minor axis b∗. Then we

<!-- b000687 | prose | pages [22] -->
further develop the 2D quarter-ellipse model into a more actual and complex case,

<!-- b000688 | prose | pages [22] -->
involving the third dimension of the halfpipe. Through numerical computation, we

<!-- b000689 | prose | pages [22] -->
ﬁnd the most suitable slope angle of the halfpipe is 17.1o. Next, the sensitivities of

<!-- b000690 | prose | pages [22] -->
diﬀerent variables are analyzed both qualitatively and quantitatively. It helps us

<!-- b000691 | prose | pages [22] -->
to compare the signiﬁcance of these variables in building a best halfpipe.

<!-- b000692 | prose | pages [22] -->
At last, the tailors and tradeoﬀs to develop a ”practical” course are discussed.

<!-- b000693 | prose | pages [22] -->
After taking construction diﬃculty, players’ safety and their performance into con-

<!-- b000694 | prose | pages [22] -->
sideration, our result becomes more practical.

<!-- b000695 | prose | pages [22] -->
In conclusion, through systematic and comprehensive analysis of various factors,

<!-- b000696 | prose | pages [22] -->
we ﬁnd a reasonable shape of the snowboard course, with a = 4.42m, b = 5.4m and

<!-- b000697 | prose | pages [22] -->
ϕ = 17.5o. Our results ﬁt the real date well.
