1	In this paper, we determine the sweet spot on a baseball bat.
2	We capture the essential physics of the ball-bat impact by taking the ball to be a lossy spring and the bat to be an Euler-Bernoulli beam.
3	In order to impart some intuition about the model we begin by presenting a rigid body model.
4	Next, we proceed to use our full model to reconcile various correct and incorrect claims about the sweet spot found in the literature.
5	Finally, we discuss the sweet spot and performance of corked and aluminum bats, with a particular emphasis on hoop modes. 1.
6	INTRODUCTION The status of baseball as the nation’s pastime makes understanding the collision of an irregularly shaped slab of wood with a ball a compelling and interesting problem.
7	The physics of baseball encompasses much more than just the batting of the ball.
8	The pitching of the ball and the flight of the ball are examples of equally important events susceptible to physics modeling.
9	Even limiting one’s scope to the batting of the ball yields a problem of many dimensions.
10	A hitter might expect a model of the baseball-bat collision to yield insight into how the bat breaks, how the bat imparts spin on the ball, how best to swing the bat, and so on.1 We model only the sweet spot.
11	We have encountered at least two notions of what the sweet spot should be defined as.
12	The first comes from what we suspect explains the term sweet spot.
13	This definition equates the sweet spot with the impact location on the bat which minimizes the discomfort to the hands.
14	We can tentatively give the second definition as the impact location which maximizes the outgoing velocity of the ball.
15	Some players seem to implicitly identify these two definitions as the same.
16	We do not identify the two and focus exclusively on the second definition, which we now define more carefully.
17	Given a baseball bat and baseball, the final velocity of the ball is determined by the initial velocity and rotation of the ball, initial velocity and rotation of the bat, the relative position and orientation of the bat and ball, and the force over time that the hitter’s hands may apply on the handle.
18	We assume that the ball is not rotating and that the ball’s velocity at impact is perpendicular to the length of the bat.
19	We assume that everything occurs in a single plane and we will argue that the hand’s interaction are actually negligible.
20	In the frame of reference of the center of mass of the bat, the initial conditions are completely specified by the angular velocity of the bat, the velocity of the ball, and the position of impact along the bat.
21	Fixing the properties of the bat, ball, initial velocity of the ball, and angular velocity, we can now define the sweet spot as the location of the point of impact which maximizes the final ball velocity.
22	In particular, the location of the sweet depends not just the bat alone, but also the pitch and swing.
23	In order to determine the actual location of the sweet spot, we model the physical effects that we find to be most important and allow that model to make a prediction.
24	Here we rapidly explain the features of a few models in the literature.
25	The simplest model predicts the sweet spot to be at the center of percussion.2 This model assumes that an impact location that minimizes discomfort to the hand also maximizes power transferred to the ball.
26	The model assumes the ball to be a rigid body for which there are conjugate points at which an impact at one will exactly balance the angular recoil and linear recoil at the other.
27	By gripping at one conjugate point and impacting at the other point, the center of percussion, the theory predicted that the hands would experience minimal shock and therefore the ball would exit with high velocity.
28	This point depends heavily on the moment of inertia and the location of the hands.
29	We cannot accept this model because it both erroneously equates the two definitions of sweet spot and furthermore assumes incorrectly that the bat is a rigid body. 1
