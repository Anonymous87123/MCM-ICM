1	We are studying the hypothetical “Big Long River,” a 225 mile wilderness river accessible only at two points, called “First Launch” and “Final Exit.” Each year, adventurers travel down the river on six to eighteen night trips, stopping at nights to camp.
2	They can either travel by motorized boats, at 8 mph, or by oar-powered boats, at 4 mph.
3	The problem is how to schedule an optimal number of trips down the river, while using the campsites most efficiently, since two boats cannot stop at the same campsite on the same night.
4	To clarify and simplify the problem, we made several assumptions.
5	We know the season is six months, so we assumed that in the most likely case, the schedule would run from April 1st to September 30th, giving us a total of 183 days to schedule (or 182 nights).
6	These months are generally warm enough in most regions for boating, and assuming them gave us a definite length for our schedule.
7	We also assumed that campsites are equally distributed.
8	In other words, if there are Y campsites, the distance between two campsites is Y225 +1 miles.
9	Another assumption is that there are at least 18 campsites.
10	More importantly, having very few campsites makes it difficult for six-night trips on oar boats to finish in time - if one particular campsite is already in use, they may not be able to travel as far as necessary to stay on schedule.
11	We assume that boats do not travel “backwards” upstream, since that would simply not make sense, and we worked that in as a rule into our scheduling algorithm.
12	Finally we assume that all trip requests are submitted well in advance of the trip’s departure date.
13	This is both reasonable and necessary for optimal scheduling, and it allows us to essentially view trip requests as an infinite queue, of which the algorithm satisfies as many requests as possible.
14	We considered several factors that go into an optimal schedule.
15	The first is simply how many trips make it down the river by the end of the season - the more, the better.
16	The second is the number of times boats pass each other.
17	Since the trips are supposed to be wilderness experiences, we would like to minimize the number of these “crossovers.” Finally, it is possible that while building a schedule, we determine that a boat will not be able to arrive at the end of the river as soon as scheduled, but instead is scheduled to arrive “late.” If the trip was specifically scheduled for a customer with real time constraints, they may not accept such a change in plans, and the schedule will have to be reworked.
18	Such reworking has a cost - either the schedule will have to undergo major rollback changes, or there will be a significant loss in efficiency.
19	The algorithm we developed for designing a schedule is priority-based.
20	By associating a priority with each boat, the algorithm ensures that high priority boats choose campsites first so that they have every chance to meet their deadline, while low priority boats basically get whatever sites are left.
21	The way we used to determine priority is to count how many days the boat has to finish its trip, and give priority to the boats with the least time left in their trip.
22	If there is a tie in this scenario, oar boats have priority over motor boats.
23	We also studied how far a boat should travel each day, and determined that we had the highest efficiency and least rescheduling when boats “paced themselves,” rather than traveling as far as possible the first few days.
24	We tested our algorithm under many conditions, varying parameters including the number of campsites, the distribution of trip lengths and boat types, and the time spent traveling each day.
25	We also determined how to calculate the theoretically optimal efficiency as a function of the number of sites: the maximum possible number of trips, M , can be found by M = 182Y , L where Y is the number of sites, and L is the average number of nights per trip.
26	We calculated this number for our best runs and compared our results against it.
27	We found that our algorithm generally had very good efficiency, as high as 98.8% under ideal circumstances, and remained efficient for nearly all likely conditions.
28	We found that our optimality peaked when we had certain “magic numbers” in our parameters.
29	Specifically, the parameters that had magic numbers were the ones that went 4h(Y +1)                 8h(Y +1) into the maximum distance equations: DO (Y ) = b 225 c and DM = b 225 c, where h is the maximum hours spent traveling per day, and Y is the number of sites.
30	The “magic” combinations of h and Y occur when DO (Y ) 6= DO (Y − 1) and DM (Y ) = 2DO (Y ) When deciding what final parameters to use when making a schedule, these must be taken into account.
31	Our model is fairly insensitive to small changes in our assumptions.
32	When we increased or decreased the number of days in the calendar, the number of trips increased linearly, which means that the efficiency remained constant.
33	Also, changing the ratio of oar boats to motor boats only affected our efficiency by about 2.5%, which is relatively insignificant.
34	Assuming either a uniform distribution or a Gaussian distribution in the desired length of trips did not affect our efficiency in any significant way either.
35	In general, our model behaved well under many different parameter combinations.
36	The strengths of our model are that it is highly efficient and minimizes rescheduling of trips.
37	Another strength is that our algorithm inherently reduces crossovers.
38	This is true because no boat will cross another that has fewer days remaining than itself.
39	One weakness in our model is that the boats are put in the water in an essentially random order (first come, first served) - we attempted to find a better way of ordering the boats, but all our attempts gave worse results than a random ordering.
40	If we were to study this problem further, we might refine the model and determine why some orderings work better than others.
41	The issue of rescheduling trips could also be improved; ideally our model could be optimized so that there would be no trips that needed to be rescheduled.
