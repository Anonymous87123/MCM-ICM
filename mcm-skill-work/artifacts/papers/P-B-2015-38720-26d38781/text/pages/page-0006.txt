       2.1 Amelia Earhart: A Crash that Haunted History
        Mankind resides in a state of perpetual discontent staying static while barriers
remain unbroken. We first achieved controlled and sustained flight on December 17,
     [1]​
1903​   . Although the Wright Brother’s flight only lasted a mere 12 seconds, the
barrier of controlled flight is one mankind sought to break from centuries.
        Aviators realized sustained flight opened a door to a land of infinite
possibilities. Amelia Earhart, renowned for her advocacy of women’s rights, became
                                                    [2]​
the first woman to fly across the Atlantic in 1928​    . Earhart’s thirst for achieving new
heights, both socially and aeronautically, became the characteristic that would bring
her both fame and, sadly, an untimely death.
        On the morning of July 2, 1937, Amelia set off to land on Howland Island, a
                                         [2]​
tiny, remote island in the south Pacific​   . Her projected flight path, an astounding
2,556 miles, was the most difficult of her career. Historians believe Earhart’s
communication antenna broke off mid flight, hindering her ability to receive
navigation instructions. Due to a series of storms, her plane ran out of fuel and she
                                                    [2]​
crashed into the dark waters of the south Pacific​     .
        Although her plane was not the first to crash in open water, the unresolved loss
of a celebrated aviation hero captured the attention the world. Earhart’s
disappearance yielded one of the largest search parties of all time. Fragments of her
                                                                       [2]​
plane were finally found last year, 77 years after her initial crash​     . Though both
airplane and search technology have improved drastically since that time, the
heartbreaking mysteries of lost planes continue to occur.
